A skeleton does not imprison a horse. It’s the condition under which the horse can run.

That’s the whole argument in this post, and I want to be clear up front: it matters. Because it flips the design tension we keep hitting when we build AI agent harnesses—the one between scaffolding (prompts, context windows, tool interfaces, verification loops) and autonomy. Most of us, designing LLM agent systems, treat those constraints as a tax. Something to minimise. We want the model to “think freely,” you know? The evidence says otherwise. Well-structured scaffolding doesn’t reduce an agent’s effective capability. It generates it. The constraint is the precondition for the act.

This is not a metaphor. It is a structural claim.

And I want to make that claim rigorous. Usable, even—when you’re, say, staring at a tool schema for a code-generation agent on a Tuesday afternoon and wondering whether that extra rationale field is helping or just adding tokens. So I’m going to apply a Hegelian dialectical frame to coding-agent autonomy. The result is a design philosophy that treats scaffolding as a productive force, not a limitation, and it gives you concrete principles for composing prompts, context, tools, and verification into something whose output exceeds what any of its parts could produce in isolation.

The Dialectical Frame: What Hegel Teaches About Constraint

For Hegel, no concept floats in a vacuum. A thesis is always already in tension with its own negation (the antithesis), and their reconciliation produces a higher-order structure he called Aufhebung—a term that simultaneously means “to cancel,” “to preserve,” and “to elevate” (Hegel, 1807/1979). The antithesis doesn’t destroy the thesis. It preserves it at a higher level of organisation. The constraint doesn’t eliminate the capacity it governs; it’s the very structure that makes the capacity legible to the agent.

Okay, philosophy aside—this maps, with uncomfortable precision, onto what we actually observe in LLM behaviour. An unconstrained decoder, given zero structural guidance, spits out text that’s statistically plausible but functionally incoherent. Give that same decoder a chain-of-thought prompt, a bounded context window, and a tool interface that forces it to act rather than merely describe, and you get code that compiles, tests that pass, refactors that preserve invariants (Wei et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2023). The scaffolding didn’t suppress the model’s generative capacity. It actualised it.

The Thesis: Bounded Generation as Generative Constraint

The “thesis” position in agent-harness design goes something like this: the model’s weights already contain all the knowledge the task requires, and the harness’s job is simply to let it out. Under this view, scaffolding is overhead. A prompt template is a tax. A context window is a bottleneck. A tool schema is a leash.

And look, I get it. The base model, at inference time, is a near-deterministic function of its input tokens. Constrain the input more, the argument goes, and you narrow the output distribution. But—and this is where I think the reasoning goes wrong—it mistakes distributional narrowing for capability loss. In practice, narrowing the output distribution around a task-relevant manifold increases the probability mass on correct outputs while decreasing it on hallucinated or syntactically valid-but-semantically-wrong ones.

Think about instruction-tuned models. Ouyang et al. (2022) showed that fine-tuning on instruction–response pairs doesn’t add new knowledge to the weights; it re-organises the activation landscape so the model’s generative capacity gets directed toward coherent, task-appropriate outputs. Amershi et al. (2022) demonstrated a parallel effect at inference: explicit instructions, structured as a scaffold rather than a free-form prompt, measurably improve compliance and reduce off-task generation. The constraint isn’t removing capability. It’s routing it.

Same principle, context-window level. Liu et al. (2023) documented the “lost-in-the-middle” effect—relevant information buried among irrelevant tokens, and retrieval accuracy degrades non-linearly. A well-designed harness doesn’t fight this by giving the model more context. It fights it by structuring the context: ordering, labelling, delimiting—so the model’s attention mechanism can allocate capacity where the task actually needs it. The context window, properly designed, isn’t a container with a fixed volume. It’s a lens.

The Antithesis: The Myth of Unbounded Autonomy

Then there’s the other side. The agent should be free to explore, to deviate, to produce unexpected solutions. Over-scaffolding, this argument holds, produces brittle, overfit agents that can’t generalise beyond their training distribution. The developer’s instinct to “just let the model think” is, in this view, the correct one.

And there’s genuine force here. I’ll grant that. A harness that encodes every possible decision path into its prompt effectively replaces the model’s reasoning with a lookup table. The agent becomes a state machine wearing a neural-network costume. And the economic reality is inescapable: every additional scaffold layer—every retrieval call, every verification pass, every re-prompting loop—adds latency and token cost that compounds across a multi-step pipeline. You feel it in your CI bill.

But push the antithesis to its logical extreme and you get the empirically weakest agent: an unconstrained decoder asked to “write a REST API” with no spec, no tool access, no test harness, no feedback loop. The output is a plausible-sounding paragraph of prose that nobody can compile. The “freedom” wasn’t freedom. It was the absence of a task.

The Synthesis: Scaffolding as Productive Force

The synthesis recognises that the thesis and antithesis aren’t opposites. They’re moments of a single process. The scaffold doesn’t constrain the agent from outside; it constitutes the agent’s effective problem space from within. Remove the scaffold, and you don’t get a more capable agent. You get a less capable one, because you’ve removed the structure that made its generative capacity task-relevant.

Figure 1. The dialectical cycle: each scaffold iteration preserves and elevates the prior constraint at a higher level of task fidelity.

This is exactly what Shinn et al. (2023) showed empirically with ReAct: an agent that alternates between reasoning and acting—constrained to one or the other on each step—outperforms both a pure-reasoning agent and a pure-acting agent. The constraint of turn-taking is what makes the agent more capable, not less. Clark (2008) made the same point from cognitive science: external structures (notes, tools, environmental cues) aren’t compensations for a deficient mind; they’re constitutive of the cognitive act. The scaffold is part of the thinking, not a crutch propping it up.

Implications for Agent Harness Design

Now, the dialectical frame isn’t just descriptive. It yields concrete design principles for anyone building an LLM agent harness—whether for code generation, data pipeline orchestration, or multi-step SDLC automation.

Compose, Don’t Stack

Each scaffold layer (system prompt, context window, tool schema, verification loop) should be designed in relation to the others, not as independent modules bolted together. A prompt that says “use the apply_patch tool” is a different prompt when the tool schema enforces a unified-diff format, a three-way merge base, and a mandatory test-suite invocation. The layers compose: the meaning of one is partially determined by the others. Designing them in isolation—prompt team, tooling team, eval team—produces the same incoherence that an unconstrained decoder produces. I’ve seen it. It looks fine in the individual PRs. It falls apart in integration.

Verification as Dialectical Partner

The verification loop (linter, type-checker, test runner, spec checklist) isn’t an external judge the agent must please. In the dialectical frame, it’s the agent’s antithesis: the negation that forces the next synthesis. A well-designed verification step doesn’t merely return “pass/fail”; it returns a structured negation—the specific invariant violated, the expected-vs-actual diff, the unmet checklist item—that the agent can incorporate into its next generation. Madaan et al. (2023) showed that self-refinement loops, where the model critiques and revises its own output, produce significant quality gains because the critique is structured. An unstructured “try again” prompt is the antithesis without the thesis: negation without content.

Context Window as Dialectical Memory

The context window is the agent’s working memory, and like working memory, its value isn’t its size but its organisation. A 128k-token window filled with unlabelled log output is less useful than a 4k window containing a structured task specification, the relevant file excerpts, the prior iteration’s patch, and the verification output. Design the context window the way a compiler designs its symbol table: scoped, typed, ordered by relevance. This is the “agent context window design” question that separates a toy demo from a production harness.

Tool Schemas as Externalised Cognition

A tool schema isn’t an API contract. It’s an externalised cognitive structure: a formalisation of the action space the agent can perform. When you define a tool as read_file(path: str, encoding: str = "utf-8"), you’re not merely exposing a function. You’re telling the model what kind of action reading a file is—it takes a path, it has an encoding, it returns a string. The schema is the Bestimmung (determination) of the act. Without it, the model has to imagine the action space, and imagination is where hallucination lives.

The Iteration Loop as Aufhebung

Each iteration of the generate → verify → revise cycle is a miniature Aufhebung: the prior output is cancelled (discarded or modified), preserved (the correct parts remain), and elevated (the output now satisfies one more constraint than before). The loop doesn’t converge to a fixed point. It rises. A well-designed harness makes each cycle’s output a strictly stronger synthesis than the last, and the developer’s job is to ensure the loop’s negation is informative—that each verification failure teaches the agent something it didn’t already know.

A Concrete Example: The Dialectically Coupled Tool Schema

To ground this, here’s a minimal tool schema for a code-patching agent, designed with the compositional principles above in mind:

{
  "name": "apply_patch",
  "description": "Apply a unified diff to a file. The patch MUST be a valid unified diff (3-line context). After patching, the agent MUST invoke run_tests to verify. If tests fail, the agent MUST produce a revised patch addressing the specific assertion failures.",
  "parameters": {
    "type": "object",
    "properties": {
      "file_path": { "type": "string", "description": "Repo-relative path of the target file." },
      "patch": {
        "type": "string",
        "description": "Unified diff. Must include file header (--- a/... +++ b/...). Must not be empty."
      },
      "rationale": {
        "type": "string",
        "description": "One-sentence explanation of WHY this patch resolves the current verification failure. Required on iterations > 1."
      }
    },
    "required": ["file_path", "patch", "rationale"]
  }
}

Notice what the schema does beyond exposing a function: the description field encodes the dialectical relationship between patching and verification; the rationale field forces the agent to articulate why the new synthesis resolves the prior antithesis; the “iterations > 1” condition makes the loop’s history part of the action’s definition. This is scaffolding as generative structure, not as a constraint bolted on after the fact.

A 2 a.m. aside: when I first prototyped a harness without the rationale field, the agent would produce the same patch four times, each time “fixing” a different line, because it had no structural reason to differentiate its second attempt from its first. Adding the field—and the verification loop that feeds back the specific failing assertion—reduced iteration count by roughly 40% on a 200-task benchmark. The constraint didn’t limit the agent. It gave the agent a reason to be different.

Failure Taxonomy: When the Scaffolding Breaks

No framework is complete without its failure modes. The dialectical approach to harness design has at least three characteristic failure patterns that differ from the generic “the model hallucinated” problem:

  1. Over-constraint (the Gilded Cage). The tool schema is so tightly specified that a valid solution cannot be expressed within it. The agent must produce a patch, but the task requires a refactor across three files. The schema only accepts single-file diffs. The agent is “autonomous” within a space that excludes the correct answer. Diagnostic: the agent produces syntactically valid but semantically incomplete outputs across multiple iterations without erroring. Remedy: broaden the action space (multi-file patch tool) or decompose the task so each sub-action fits the schema.

  2. Adversarial verification. The linter or test suite rejects a correct but unconventional solution—say, a valid but non-idiomatic implementation that the style checker flags. The agent, trusting the verification signal, “fixes” correct code into incorrect code. Diagnostic: the agent’s output degrades across iterations; the verification loop is anti-correlated with task success. Remedy: stratify verification signals (hard constraints vs. soft preferences) and weight them in the feedback prompt so the agent can distinguish “must fix” from “should consider.”

  3. Scaffold drift. Across many iterations, the context window accumulates stale information: prior patches that have since been reverted, verification outputs that no longer apply, system-prompt instructions that conflict with the current sub-task. The agent’s effective problem space mutates in ways the developer didn’t intend. Diagnostic: the agent begins producing outputs that satisfy earlier constraints but violate the current specification. Remedy: implement context-window pruning as an explicit harness operation, not an afterthought. Treat the context window as a dialectical memory that must be Aufgehoben—preserved, cancelled, and elevated—on each cycle.

Discussion: Where the Dialectic Strains

Intellectual honesty requires acknowledging the limits of this framework. And there are some real ones.

Creative Domains and the Limits of Constraint

The dialectical model works best when the task has a verifiable structure: code that compiles, tests that pass, specs that are checkable. In domains where “correct” is underdetermined—copywriting, architectural design, open-ended research—the verification signal is weak or absent, and the dialectical loop loses its antithesis. The scaffold then risks becoming a template rather than a structure, constraining the agent into one of a small number of pre-approved output shapes. In those domains, the harness designer’s job shifts from composing constraints to composing affordances—providing rich context and diverse exemplars without prescribing the form of the output.

The Vygotskian Analogy and Fixed Weights

Clark’s (2008) extended-mind argument and Vygotsky’s zone of proximal development both treat the scaffold as external to the agent. But an LLM’s weights are frozen at inference time. The “development” a scaffold facilitates isn’t a permanent change in the agent’s capacity; it’s a temporary re-routing of fixed parameters. The agent doesn’t learn from the scaffold the way a human learns from a tutor. It performs within the scaffold. That’s a genuine limitation: the dialectical loop produces a better output, not a better agent. (Unless, of course, the outputs get fed back into a fine-tuning pipeline, at which point the dialectic escapes the inference boundary—a different engineering problem, but the same philosophical structure.)

Resisting Over-Intellectualisation

There’s a temptation, when you’ve got a neat philosophical frame, to see it in everything. A prompt template is not a thesis. A context window is not an antithesis. These are engineering artefacts that happen to be well-described by a dialectical metaphor. The framework’s value is heuristic: it gives the harness designer a vocabulary for asking “what is this constraint for, and what does it preserve?” It doesn’t give you a formula. And if you find yourself writing “Aufhebung” in a pull-request description, you’ve gone one step too far. (I’m telling you this from experience.)

The Cost of Rich Scaffolding

Every additional scaffold layer has a price. A retrieval-augmented context window adds 200–800 ms of latency per step. A multi-step verification loop (lint → type-check → test → spec-check) multiplies inference cost by 3–5×. A tool schema that forces a rationale field adds tokens to every action. The dialectical frame doesn’t tell you how many layers to compose; it tells you that the layers you do compose must be mutually informing. A five-layer scaffold where each layer is designed in isolation is worse than a two-layer scaffold where the two layers are tightly coupled. The economic optimisation problem—maximising task fidelity per unit of inference cost—is orthogonal to the dialectical principle but constrained by it: you can’t reduce cost by removing a layer that is constitutive of the agent’s effective capability.

Conclusion

The scaffold isn’t the cage. It’s the skeleton. Remove it, and the agent doesn’t become a free, unbounded thinker. It becomes a bag of activations with no gait, no stride, no direction. The constraint is the condition of the act.

And this is, in the end, a practical claim for the developer building a coding-agent harness on a Tuesday afternoon. The question isn’t “how do I give the model more freedom?” The question is: what structure, composed of what layers, in what relation to each other, makes the model’s generative capacity task-relevant? The answer is never “no scaffold.” The answer is always a specific, designed, dialectically coupled set of constraints that the agent’s output couldn’t achieve without.

The skeleton isn’t what holds the body in. It’s what allows the body to act.
