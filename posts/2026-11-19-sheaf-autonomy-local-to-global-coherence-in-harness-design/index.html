Prerequisites. Fair warning: this post assumes you’ve at least skimmed basic sheaf theory—sections, restriction maps, the sheaf axioms—and you’re comfortable with Čech cohomology in low degrees. If that’s not your thing, no problem. Mac Lane & Moerdijk, Sheaves in Geometry and Logic, §§ II.1–III.2, gives you a solid refresher, or the nLab entry on sheaves will get you oriented in about ten minutes. And no, you do not need algebraic geometry. I promise.

Abstract

Let’s get the practical upshot out of the way first. Here’s the thing that actually matters: a multi-module coding-agent harness can pass every single pairwise interface test and still be structurally incapable of end-to-end autonomy. And the failure is topological, not parametric. You can’t prompt your way out of it. You can’t nudge a threshold. Nothing local will fix what’s broken at the level of composition.

This post gives you two things: a CI-auditable diagnostic that catches the obstruction in O(n³) time, and a repair algorithm that patches it with minimal perturbation to the constraint set.

The underlying idea is a recast of the local-to-global coherence problem in agent harness design as a question in sheaf theory. Each module’s capability set becomes a section of a presheaf over a finite task-topology. The sheaf gluing axiom, in turn, becomes the necessary-and-sufficient condition for locally autonomous patches to compose into a globally coherent, fully autonomous system (Mac Lane & Moerdijk, 1992, §II.1).

Formally, we derive a computable first Čech-cohomology diagnostic—H¹(X, F)—that flags structurally incoherent harnesses. Then we present a sheafification algorithm that repairs the incoherence with provably minimal perturbation to the constraint set. Both are implementable as a standard CI gate. No LLM inference at runtime. None.

Key Takeaways

  • Agent harness coherence is a higher-order property. It constrains the composition of compatible interfaces, not merely pairwise agreement. Your standard type-checkers and contract verifiers simply cannot see it.
  • A nonzero H¹ class is a topological obstruction. No local reparameterization of module prompts or thresholds can eliminate it. That’s the whole point.
  • The sheafification repair is unique up to isomorphism and perturbs the constraint set minimally. In practice, that means it’s safe to roll out to production.
  • The entire diagnostic is a pure algebraic computation over finite sets. It runs in CI in milliseconds. Zero model inference, zero stochastic sampling.

1. Motivation: The Local-to-Global Gap in Multi-Agent Harnesses

Picture a three-module autonomous coding agent. You’ve got a Planner that decomposes a task, an Executor that generates and applies code patches, and a Verifier that checks invariants and test suites. Each module is locally autonomous—can act, self-correct, escalate within its own capability envelope. Pairwise interface tests pass: Planner output parses as Executor input, Executor output parses as Verifier input. Green across the board.

And yet the composed system fails.

Here’s how it goes wrong. The Planner emits a refactoring plan that the Executor can implement—only if the Verifier’s invariant set is relaxed. The Verifier, meanwhile, demands a post-condition the Planner never emits. You look at each module in isolation and they’re fine. The triad is not.

Now, is this a prompt-engineering problem? No. A sampling-temperature problem? Also no. It’s something more fundamental. In the language of algebraic topology, it’s a non-vanishing first cohomology class: a global obstruction that no local adjustment can cancel. You’re stuck.

The prior posts in this series have attacked harness autonomy from every angle I could find—optimal transport, spectral analysis, information geometry, mean-field games. Each one gives you a quantitative handle: bounds on refactoring cost, convergence rates, curvature drift. Useful, sure. But what they don’t give you is a qualitative gate. A hard yes/no test that says, “this harness, as structured, cannot be made fully autonomous by any amount of local tuning.” That’s the role of sheaf cohomology. And it’s the gap this post is here to fill.

2. The Sheaf-Theoretic Recast

2.1 From Modules to Sections

Let X be the finite set of task types the harness must handle (think bug-fix, feature-add, refactor, test-gen). Equip X with the discrete topology τ = P(X). In the finite case this is really the only non-trivial choice, and it keeps all cohomology computations finite, which is exactly what you want when you’re running this in CI.

For each module m in the harness, define a presheaf F_m : τ^op → Set as follows:

  • F_m(U) = the set of capability states module m can realize on the sub-collection U ⊆ X of task types.
  • For V ⊆ U, the restriction map res^U_V : F_m(U) → F_m(V) is just “forget the task types in U  V.”

A section s ∈ F(X) = F(X) is then a globally consistent assignment of capability states across all task types, respecting restrictions. The sheaf condition (Mac Lane & Moerdijk, 1992, §II.1; Bredon, 1993, Topology and Geometry, §II.4) demands:

If {U_i} is an open cover of U and s_i ∈ F(U_i) agree on all overlaps U_i ∩ U_j, then there exists a unique s ∈ F(U) restricting to each s_i.

In harness terms, this is the formalization of “local autonomy patches compose into global autonomy.” If every pair of modules agrees on their shared task types, and every triple agrees on their shared task types, then a globally coherent autonomous assignment must exist. The sheaf condition is the precise statement of that.

2.2 Why This Is Not (Just) a Type Checker

I can almost hear the objection: “Wait, isn’t this just interface compatibility checking?” It’s not. And the distinction is critical, so let me be precise.

Standard type-checking and contract verification—Zephyr, Pact, OpenAPI conformance, whatever your stack uses—enforce pairwise agreement. Module A’s output type matches module B’s input type. Done. That’s the 0th-order sheaf condition: agreement on single overlaps.

The sheaf condition is higher-order. It constrains the composition of compatible interfaces across all overlaps simultaneously. Two modules can agree pairwise (types match, contracts hold) yet the triple composition violate a global invariant. That’s exactly the Planner/Executor/Verifier failure above: every pair is type-compatible, but the triad is not a section.

In categorical terms, a type checker verifies that a diagram of two objects and one morphism commutes. The sheaf condition verifies that every finite sub-diagram in the nerve of the task-topology commutes. The gap between those two is exactly H¹.

3. Čech Cohomology: Computing the H¹ Incoherence Obstruction

3.1 The Cech Complex

Given a finite cover {U_1, …, U_n} of X and the presheaf F (here, the intersection presheaf F = ∩ F_m, capturing the joint capability constraint), the first Čech cochain complex is:

C⁰ = ⊕i F(U_i), C¹ = ⊕{i<j} F(U_i ∩ U_j), C² = ⊕_{i<j<k} F(U_i ∩ U_j ∩ U_k)

with the standard coboundary δ⁰ : C⁰ → C¹ and δ¹ : C¹ → C². The first cohomology group is:

H¹(X, F) = ker(δ¹) / im(δ⁰)

A nonzero class [σ] ∈ H¹(X, F) is a closed 1-cochain that is not a coboundary. In plain English: a cyclic agreement pattern among modules that you simply cannot “unwind” into any global section. That’s the incoherence obstruction. The thing you can’t prompt away.

3.2 Worked Example: Planner / Executor / Verifier

Let X = {bug-fix, refactor, test-gen} with the discrete cover U₁ = {bug-fix, refactor}, U₂ = {refactor, test-gen}, U₃ = {bug-fix, test-gen}.

Define the joint capability presheaf F by the following table (entries are capability-state labels):

Section U₁ = {bf, rf} U₂ = {rf, tg} U₃ = {bf, tg}
s₁ (A, B) — —
s₂ — (B, C) —
s₃ — — (C, A)

Restriction maps (on overlaps): - U₁ ∩ U₂ = {refactor}: s₁|{rf} = B, s₂|{rf} = B ✓ (agree) - U₂ ∩ U₃ = {test-gen}: s₂|{tg} = C, s₃|{tg} = C ✓ (agree) - U₃ ∩ U₁ = {bug-fix}: s₃|{bf} = A, s₁|{bf} = A ✓ (agree)

So the 1-cochain σ = (s₂ − s₁, s₃ − s₂, s₁ − s₃) has δ¹σ = 0 (all triple overlaps agree trivially in the discrete case). But σ is not a coboundary: there is no 0-cochain (global section) that restricts to s₁, s₂, s₃ simultaneously, because the labels A → B → C → A form a non-trivial cycle with no consistent global assignment.

H¹(X, F) ≅ ℤ/3ℤ (or a nontrivial subgroup, depending on the exact set structure). The harness is structurally incoherent. And no amount of prompt tuning on the Planner is going to fix it. I know that sounds harsh, but it’s true.

3.3 Computational Complexity

For a finite cover of n sets and |F(U_i)| ≤ k states per module, the Čech computation runs in O(n³ · k²) time and O(n² · k) space. For a typical 3–7 module harness, that’s a sub-millisecond computation. No LLM inference. No stochastic sampling. No runtime model calls. Just set algebra.

4. Sheafification: Minimal Perturbation Repair

4.1 The Sheafification Theorem

The sheafification F⁺ of a presheaf F is the unique (up to unique isomorphism) sheaf with a natural map F → F⁺ that is universal among all sheaf maps out of F (Bredon, 1993, Topology and Geometry, §II.5; Hartshorne, 1977, II.5.15).

What does that mean in harness terms? Sheafification is the minimal modification of the capability-state sets and restriction maps that restores the gluing axiom. It adds no new task types. It removes no modules. It perturbs the constraint sets by the least possible amount. That last part is doing a lot of work, because in practice it means you’re not rewriting your harness—you’re making the smallest edit that unblocks the global composition.

4.2 The Algorithm

SHEAFIFY(F, cover {U_i}):
  1. For each U in cover, compute F⁺(U) = colim_{V ⊇ U} F(V)
     (stabilize capability states under refinement)
  2. For each inclusion V ⊆ U, set res^U_V = canonical colimit map
  3. Verify: for every cover {V_j} of U, the natural map
     ⊕_j F⁺(V_j) → F⁺(U) is an isomorphism
  4. Return (F⁺, res maps, certification witness)

Step 3 is the CI assertion—the one that actually goes red or green. The certification witness (a finite set of explicit section equalities) is your proof certificate that the repaired harness satisfies the sheaf axiom. You can inspect it. You can diff it. It’s not a black box.

4.3 Minimality Guarantee

The colimit construction in Step 1 is the freest sheaf extending F. Any other repair G with F → G factors uniquely through F⁺. In constraint-set terms: if a harness engineer manually adjusts capability states to fix the H¹ obstruction, the sheafification is the smallest such adjustment. And that matters for production safety, because minimal perturbation minimizes regression risk. You’re not shaking up the whole system. You’re making the one edit that’s actually necessary.

5. Implementing a Sheaf Coherence Gate in CI/CD

The entire diagnostic is a pure algebraic computation over finite sets. It slots naturally into an existing CI pipeline as a pre-deployment gate:

Figure 1. The sheaf-coherence CI pipeline, from presheaf input to verified sheaf output.

Practical integration notes:

  • Input format. Each module’s capability spec is a YAML file listing its supported task types and the state labels it can realize on each. Restriction maps are declared as bijections or inclusions between state sets. A 3-module harness needs about 15 lines of YAML. That’s it.
  • CI invocation. harness-sheaf check --cover cover.yaml --specs planner.yaml executor.yaml verifier.yaml. Exit code 0 = sheaf (pass); exit code 1 = nonzero H¹ (fail, with diagnostic); exit code 2 = sheafification patch available (optional auto-fix).
  • Reference implementation. See the harness-sheaf package (Python 3.10+, pip install harness-sheaf). The core Čech computation is roughly 200 lines of pure-Python set algebra; the sheafification colimit is about 80 lines. No ML dependencies. None at all.
  • Interaction with prior gates. This gate is orthogonal to the spectral-gap check (convergence rate), the free-energy bound (per-step KL budget), and the ergodicity certificate (mixing time). All four can run in parallel in a single CI stage. The sheaf gate is the only one that’s a hard yes/no structural test; the others are quantitative bounds. They answer different questions.

6. Discussion

A few connections here that I think merit being spelled out.

Mechanism design. The sheafification minimal-perturbation property is the sheaf-theoretic analogue of the revelation principle in mechanism design (Myerson, 1979): the “truthful” repair is the one that reveals the least new structure. In the companion post on incentive-compatible harness design, the Pareto-optimal constraint allocation is precisely the sheafification when the module interaction graph is a tree. For general graphs, the H¹ obstruction measures the gap between the tree-optimum and the graph-necessary constraint. I find that connection genuinely satisfying.

“The sheaf condition is what mechanism design’s revelation principle does for agent capability: it says the only coherent global assignment is the one that every local module already agrees on. There is no hidden global state to optimize. There is only the question of whether the local agreements are consistent enough to glue.”

Comparison to the cohomological obstruction post. The earlier result in Cohomological Obstructions to Complete Harness Autonomy proved the existence of topological obstructions in general. Existence is nice. It’s also somewhat abstract. The present post makes the obstruction computable and repairable: H¹ is not merely a philosophical barrier but a finite-dimensional linear algebra problem over a finite set, with a canonical repair (sheafification) and a CI-executable certificate. That shift from “there exists an obstruction” to “here’s the number, here’s the fix, here’s the proof” is the whole point.

Relation to gauge symmetry. The gauge-symmetry framework identifies autonomy-preserving relabelings of module state spaces. Sheafification is invariant under these gauge transformations: if F and F′ are gauge-equivalent presheaves, then F⁺ ≅ (F′)⁺. What that means in practice is that the H¹ diagnostic is gauge-invariant—it detects structural incoherence independent of the arbitrary labeling of capability states. You can rename your states however you like; the topology doesn’t care.

Limitations. I’ll be upfront: the framework, as presented, handles abelian section spaces (sets with a distinguished basepoint, or abelian groups). Multi-round agent loops with non-commutative state composition—where the order of “plan → execute → verify → replan” actually matters—require non-abelian sheaf cohomology. There, H¹ is only a pointed set, not a group, and the sheafification theorem needs modification. I’m flagging this as the subject of a forthcoming post. You’re going to want to read that one.

7. Conclusion

The sheaf gluing axiom is not an aesthetic formalization. It’s not a way to make the theory look prettier. It is the exact necessary-and-sufficient condition for local module autonomy to compose into global harness autonomy, and its failure is detectable by a finite, deterministic, CI-executable computation. The H¹ obstruction is the structural signature of harnesses that no amount of prompt engineering, threshold tuning, or pairwise interface repair can make fully autonomous. And sheafification provides the minimal, provably safe repair.

In the next post in this series, we extend the framework to non-abelian section spaces and derive a higher-cohomology obstruction (H²) for multi-round agent loops, where the composition order of module actions introduces a genuinely non-commutative coherence constraint. We’ll also connect the sheaf-coherence gate to the percolation critical-density heuristic to show how constraint density and topological obstruction interact near the autonomy phase transition.
