How fast does your coding-agent harness actually converge to full autonomy? I mean, really—how fast? Because if you’re debugging a harness that stalls at 70 % task completion, keeps oscillating between tool calls, or quietly regresses after some innocent prompt tweak, the answer is almost never “the model is bad.” (I know, it’s tempting to blame the model. I’ve been there.) More often, it’s the harness—that orchestration layer wrapping your coding LLM in tool dispatch, verification loops, safety guardrails—that’s got a structural bottleneck no amount of prompt engineering is going to fix. This post is about measuring that bottleneck. Precisely.

Here’s the deal. We model the harness as a discrete-time dynamical system acting on a high-dimensional behavior space, linearize its autonomy operator, and pull out a spectral gap Δ: the difference between the two dominant eigenvalues. Δ gives you a rigorous, computable lower bound on how many ticks it takes to go from “just started” to “fully autonomous.” Then we characterize which eigenmodes are fragile under harness perturbation, derive Lipschitz sensitivity certificates you can actually check before deployment (not after the 3 a.m. incident), and propose a spectral-conditioning algorithm that maximizes Δ under safety constraints.

TL;DR / Quick-Start. Need the diagnostic and nothing else? Log a 50-tick state trace from your harness, form the linearized operator A, run np.linalg.eigvals(A), sort by magnitude, and read off Δ = |λ₁| − |λ₂|. If Δ < 0.1, your harness is going to take > 20 ticks to settle (and you’re about to have a bad CI day). If a single eigenmode has a condition number κ > 10, some small prompt or tool-config change is going to destabilize it, and retuning another knob won’t save you. Section 7 walks through the full workflow. Should take you an afternoon, tops.

1. Why Convergence Speed Is the Right Metric

Back in the five-level autonomy spectrum we laid out earlier, “full autonomy” isn’t a binary switch you flip. It’s an attractor. The harness asymptotically hands more decision authority to the LLM as verification confidence accumulates. So the practical question for anyone building a harness isn’t “does it reach autonomy?” (it does, eventually, if you’re patient). The question is “how many ticks does it take, and is that rate robust to the changes I’m about to ship?”

The rate-distortion analysis gave us a cost floor: the minimum information bandwidth a harness must burn to sustain a given autonomy level. Necessary? Yes. Sufficient? No. Two harnesses with identical rate-distortion profiles can converge at wildly different speeds—one in 5 ticks, the other in 50—purely because of the eigenstructure of their internal dynamics. The spectral gap Δ is exactly the quantity that separates those two cases.

And this matters for coding-agent harness convergence in three very concrete ways:

  • CI/CD gating. Small Δ means your harness is going to oscillate across sprint boundaries. Flaky integration results. You know the pain.
  • Prompt regression. A one-line system-prompt change is a perturbation δA. If the dominant eigenmode is ill-conditioned, that tiny nudge can flip the harness from convergent to divergent. Oof.
  • Safety auditing. A small Δ means the harness spends a lot of ticks in that “partially autonomous” limbo—neither fully supervised nor fully self-governing. That’s precisely the zone where cohomological obstructions love to hide.

Okay. The rest of this post builds the machinery. Let’s get into it.

2. The Harness as a Discrete-Time System

2.1 State space and norm

Let \(\mathcal{B} \subset \mathbb{R}^d\) be the behavior space of a single harness tick. A typical \(d\) includes confidence scores (\(\in[0,1]\)), cumulative token counts (integers), binary tool-call flags, and verification-pass indicators. Now, here’s something people gloss over: because those components carry mixed units and ranges, the choice of norm is not cosmetic. It actually matters.

Convention. Unless otherwise stated, all norms below are the weighted \(\ell_2\) norm \(\|x\|_W = \sqrt{x^\top W x}\) where \(W = \mathrm{diag}(w_1,\dots,w_d)\) is a fixed positive-definite weighting matrix chosen so that each component contributes comparably (e.g., \(w_i = 1/\sigma_i^2\) where \(\sigma_i\) is the empirical standard deviation of component \(i\) over a calibration trace). All spectral statements are invariant under this change of inner product; only the numerical condition numbers depend on \(W\).

2.2 The autonomy operator

Define the autonomy operator \(\Phi: \mathcal{B} \to \mathcal{B}\) by

\[b_{n+1} = \Phi(b_n),\]

where \(b_n\) is the behavior vector at tick \(n\). In practice, \(\Phi\) is a black-box composition: prompt construction → LLM forward pass → tool dispatch → verification → state update. You don’t need a closed form. You need local linearity. That’s it.

Stochastic clarification. Real LLMs are stochastic (temperature \(> 0\), sampling, tool-call retries—yeah, all of it). The linearization below applies to the mean-field operator \(\bar{\Phi} = \mathbb{E}[\Phi \mid b_n]\), i.e., the expected next state averaged over the sampling distribution. In practice, you estimate \(\bar{A}\) from the time-average of per-tick state deltas over a long run (see §7.2). Per-tick variance around this mean gets treated as additive noise and doesn’t affect the eigenvalue spectrum to first order, provided the variance is bounded (which it is for any finite-temperature decoder. Don’t worry about it.)

2.3 Linearization

Assume \(b^*\) is a fixed point of \(\Phi\) (the full-autonomy state, or more practically, the steady-state attractor). The linearized autonomy operator is

\[A = D\Phi(b^*) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d},\]

the Jacobian of \(\Phi\) evaluated at \(b^*\). If \(\rho(A) < 1\) (spectral radius strictly below one), \(b^*\) is locally asymptotically stable and the system converges. The rate of convergence is governed by the spectrum of \(A\). Simple as that.

In practice you never compute \(D\Phi\) analytically (I’ve never seen anyone do it, honestly). Instead, you estimate \(A\) from a finite trace:

\[A \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \frac{(b_{n+1} - b^*)(b_{n+1} - b^*)^\top}{\|b_n - b^*\|^2}\]

or, more robustly, via a least-squares fit \(b_{n+1} - b^* \approx A(b_n - b^*)\) over \(N \geq 50\) steady-state ticks.

3. Spectral Decomposition and the Gap

3.1 Eigenmodes

Let the eigenvalues of \(A\) be \(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_d\) ordered so that \(|\lambda_1| \geq |\lambda_2| \geq \cdots \geq |\lambda_d|\). The right eigenvectors \(\{v_i\}\) span the behavior space (assuming \(A\) is diagonalizable; the defective case is handled by Jordan blocks and doesn’t change the convergence-rate bound, so let’s not go there).

The spectral gap is

\[\Delta = |\lambda_1| - |\lambda_2|.\]

Here’s the intuition. \(\lambda_1\) governs the “fast mode”—immediate tool dispatch, syntax validation, the stuff that should happen instantly. \(\lambda_2\) governs the “slow mode”—cross-file reasoning, architectural consistency checks. The gap Δ measures how cleanly those two timescales separate. If they’re bunched together, you’ve got a problem.

Key result. If \(\rho(A) < 1\) and \(A\) is diagonalizable with condition number \(\kappa(A) = \|\Phi_A\|\,\|\Phi_A^{-1}\|\) (where \(\Phi_A\) is the eigenvector matrix), then for any initial state \(b_0\):

\[\|b_n - b^*\|_W \leq C\,\kappa(A)\,\left(|\lambda_2| + \varepsilon\right)^n\]

where \(C\) depends on \(\|b_0 - b^*\|_W\) and \(\varepsilon > 0\) is arbitrary. The number of ticks to \(\delta\)-convergence is bounded below by

\[n^* \geq \frac{\log(\delta / \|b_0 - b^*\|_W)}{\log(|\lambda_2|)}.\]

In plain English: your harness is going to take at least \(n^*\) ticks to settle. And if Δ is small (meaning \(|\lambda_2|\) is close to \(|\lambda_1|\)), that lower bound is large and the convergence curve stays “flat” for a long stretch. That’s the regime where your CI pipeline starts seeing flaky results and someone files a ticket at 2 p.m. on a Friday.

3.2 A 3 × 3 toy example

Suppose \(d = 3\) and

\[A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.95 & 0.02 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.82 & 0.01 \\ 0 & 0 & 0.40 \end{pmatrix}.\]

Eigenvalues: \(\lambda_1 = 0.95\), \(\lambda_2 = 0.82\), \(\lambda_3 = 0.40\).

  • \(\Delta = 0.95 - 0.82 = 0.13.\)
  • \(\kappa(A) \approx 1.8\) (nearly orthogonal eigenvectors; well-conditioned—nice and clean).
  • \(n^* \geq \log(10^{-3}/0.5)/\log(0.82) \approx 7.7\) ticks to reach \(0.1\%\) residual.

Now perturb \(A\) by changing the \((1,2)\) entry from 0.02 to 0.15 (a more aggressive cross-component coupling):

\[A' = \begin{pmatrix} 0.95 & 0.15 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.82 & 0.01 \\ 0 & 0 & 0.40 \end{pmatrix}.\]

Eigenvalues shift to \(\lambda_1' \approx 0.96\), \(\lambda_2' \approx 0.81\). The gap widens slightly, which seems fine at first glance. But here’s the catch: the eigenvector for \(\lambda_1\) now has a large component along axis 2. The “fast mode” has absorbed part of the slow-mode behavior. If you later remove the coupling (revert to 0.02), the eigenvalues shift back and the mode shapes swap—any downstream logic keyed to “axis 1 = fast” is going to misbehave during that transition. This is the fragility we formalize next. And it’s subtle enough that you won’t catch it in a unit test.

3.3 Reading the eigenmode table

Mode \(\|\lambda_i\|\) Interpretation Typical harness component
\(v_1\) 0.95 Fast: immediate syntactic validation, single-file edit dispatch LLM forward pass + parser
\(v_2\) 0.82 Slow: cross-reference resolution, multi-file consistency Verification loop, test harness
\(v_3\) 0.40 Transient: initial prompt encoding, context-window warmup Tokenization, system-prompt injection

Small gap between rows 1 and 2? Your fast and slow loops are entangled. Large gap? You can parallelize them. That’s the whole read.

4. Perturbation, Fragility, and the Parametric–Structural Divide

4.1 First-order eigenvalue sensitivity

By Kato’s perturbation theorem (Kato, 1966; see also the treatment in Davies, 1996), if \(\delta A\) is a small perturbation with \(\|\delta A\| < \Delta/2\), then each eigenvalue \(\lambda_i\) shifts by

\[|\lambda_i' - \lambda_i| \leq \kappa_i \|\delta A\| + O(\|\delta A\|^2),\]

where \(\kappa_i = \frac{\|v_i\|_W\,\|u_i\|_W}{|\langle u_i, v_i\rangle_W|}\) is the condition number of eigenmode \(i\) (here \(u_i\) is the corresponding left eigenvector, normalized so that \(\langle u_i, v_j\rangle_W = \delta_{ij}\)).

What this means for your Tuesday-afternoon debugging session: you change a single hyperparameter (temperature, max-tokens, a tool timeout—something small, something that shouldn’t matter), and your harness suddenly oscillates. Check whether the eigenmode you disturbed has \(\kappa_i > 5\). If it does, the perturbation was structurally destabilizing, not just numerically inconvenient. Reverting the change is the only fix. Retuning another parameter won’t help. Trust me on this one.

4.2 Parametric vs. structural fragility

“A parametrically fragile mode can be re-tuned; a structurally fragile mode must be re-architected.”

Parametric fragility (\(\kappa_i\) moderate, \(\Delta\) small): the eigenvalues are close but the eigenvectors are well-conditioned. You can widen the gap by adjusting a single scalar (e.g., increasing the verification-loop gain). This is the regime where the free-energy budget gives you a per-step KL cap to work within. You’re in luck here.

Structural fragility (\(\kappa_i\) large): the right and left eigenvectors are nearly orthogonal, meaning the mode is “hidden” in the state space. No scalar retune is going to save you. You have to change the topology of the harness—add or remove a feedback loop, split a monolithic verification step into two, restructure the tool-dispatch graph. This is precisely the regime where cohomological obstructions predict that no continuous deformation of the harness will fix the failure. And you’ll feel it in your soul when you hit it.

The distinction between these two is honestly the single most useful diagnostic in this entire post. Before reaching for a new prompt, ask yourself: is my fragile mode parametric or structural? The answer determines whether you need a 10-minute fix or a two-week refactor.

4.3 Relation to the percolation picture

The percolation framework identifies a critical constraint density \(\rho_c\) below which autonomy percolates across the harness. The spectral gap provides the rate at which that percolation completes: near \(\rho_c\), Δ → 0 and convergence is algebraic rather than exponential (much slower, much more painful). The two frameworks are complementary. Percolation tells you whether autonomy emerges; the spectral gap tells you how fast it does.

5. Lipschitz Sensitivity Certificates

5.1 The certificate

Theorem (Sensitivity Certificate). Let \(A\) be the linearized autonomy operator at \(b^*\), with spectral gap Δ and maximum eigenmode condition number \(\kappa_{\max} = \max_i \kappa_i\). For any perturbation \(\delta A\) with \(\|\delta A\|_W \leq \eta < \Delta/(2\kappa_{\max})\):

  1. \(A + \delta A\) remains diagonalizable.
  2. All eigenvalues remain inside the unit disk if \(\rho(A) + \eta < 1\).
  3. The convergence rate degrades by at most a factor of \((1 + 2\kappa_{\max}\eta/\Delta)^{-1}\) relative to the unperturbed rate.

Proof sketch. Items (1) and (2) follow from standard matrix perturbation bounds (see Horn & Johnson, 2013, Ch. 6). Item (3) is the actual content: the slow-mode eigenvalue \(|\lambda_2|\) shifts by at most \(\kappa_2 \eta\), so the new convergence constant is \(|\lambda_2| + \kappa_2\eta\), giving the stated factor. \(\square\)

Key intuition (callout). Think of this as the spectral twin of the free-energy KL budget. The KL budget says “you may spend at most \(\epsilon\) bits of surprise per tick.” The sensitivity certificate says “you may perturb the harness by at most \(\eta\) in operator norm without losing more than X % of your convergence rate.” Both are pre-deployment checks. You compute them before shipping, not after the incident. (I can’t stress this enough—compute them before.)

5.2 Computing the certificate

In practice, it goes like this:

  1. Form \(A\) from your trace (§7.2).
  2. Compute eigenvalues and eigenvectors: vals, vecs = np.linalg.eig(A).
  3. Compute left eigenvectors: uvecs = np.linalg.inv(vecs).T.
  4. For each mode \(i\), compute \(\kappa_i = \|v_i\|_W \|u_i\|_W / |\langle u_i, v_i\rangle_W|\).
  5. Report \(\Delta\), \(\kappa_{\max}\), and the admissible perturbation budget \(\eta^* = \Delta/(2\kappa_{\max})\).

If \(\eta^* < 10^{-3}\), your harness is in the structurally fragile regime. Do not ship any prompt or tool-config change without a full re-trace. I’m being serious here.

6. A Spectral-Conditioning Algorithm

The goal: given a harness with linearized operator \(A\) and a safety constraint set \(\mathcal{S}\) (e.g., “no tool may execute without a verification pass”), find a perturbation \(\delta A\) that maximizes Δ subject to \(\|\delta A\|_W \leq \eta_{\text{budget}}\) and \(A + \delta A \in \mathcal{S}\).

Figure 1: Spectral-conditioning workflow. The decision at node C routes structurally fragile harnesses to topological analysis rather than scalar retuning.

The convex program in node F is a semidefinite program (SDP) because the constraint “Δ(A + δA) ≥ t” can be written as a linear matrix inequality in the eigenvalue ordering. Standard solvers (CVX, MOSEK) handle \(d \leq 200\) in seconds. For larger \(d\), restrict δA to a low-rank subspace (e.g., only the tool-dispatch coupling matrix is tunable) and solve the reduced problem. It’ll still work.

Safety constraint encoding. Each guardrail in \(\mathcal{S}\) becomes a linear inequality on the entries of δA. For example, “verification must gate every write-tool call” translates to \(\delta A_{\text{write},\text{verif}} \leq 0\) (the perturbation may not weaken the verification→write coupling). Straightforward once you see it.

This algorithm complements the Wilsonian RG cascade for identifying which constraints to relax. RG tells you the fixed-point structure; spectral conditioning tells you the optimal direction to move along the constraint manifold. They’re two sides of the same coin.

7. Practical Workflow: From Trace to Certificate

This is the section to bookmark. Seriously. Everything above is the why; this is the how, and it’s the part you’ll actually run on a Tuesday.

7.1 Prerequisites

  • A harness in (near) steady-state operation.
  • A state logger that records \(b_n\) at every tick. Minimum fields: confidence score, token count, active tool, verification-pass boolean, number of pending tasks.
  • Python with numpy, scipy, and (for the SDP) cvxpy.

7.2 Estimating \(b^*\) and \(A\)

Identifying \(b^*\). Run the harness on a representative task for \(T \geq 200\) ticks. Take the time-average of the last 50 ticks:

\[\hat{b}^* = \frac{1}{50}\sum_{n=T-49}^{T} b_n.\]

Check convergence: if \(\|b_T - \hat{b}^*\|_W > 0.05\,\|\hat{b}^*\|_W\), your harness hasn’t reached steady state. Bump up \(T\) or reduce task complexity. Don’t skip this step.

Estimating \(A\). Form the regression problem:

\[\min_A \sum_{n=1}^{N} \|b_{n+1} - \hat{b}^* - A(b_n - \hat{b}^*)\|_W^2\]

with \(N \geq 50\) ticks from the steady-state window. Solve via scipy.linalg.lstsq. Done.

7.3 Computing the diagnostic

import numpy as np

vals, vecs = np.linalg.eig(A)
order = np.argsort(np.abs(vals))[::-1]
vals, vecs = vals[order], vecs[:, order]

delta = np.abs(vals[0]) - np.abs(vals[1])
print(f"Spectral gap Δ = {delta:.4f}")

# Condition numbers
uvecs = np.linalg.inv(vecs).T
kappas = []
for i in range(d):
    ki = np.linalg.norm(vecs[:,i]) * np.linalg.norm(uvecs[i,:]) / abs(np.dot(uvecs[i,:], vecs[:,i]))
    kappas.append(ki)
kappa_max = max(kappas)
print(f"Max eigenmode κ = {kappa_max:.2f}")
print(f"Admissible perturbation budget η* = {delta / (2*kappa_max):.2e}")

7.4 Interpreting the output

Δ κ_max Diagnosis Action
> 0.15 < 5 Healthy No action; monitor
0.05–0.15 < 5 Slow but stable Consider spectral conditioning (§6)
< 0.05 < 5 Near-critical Urgent: convergence will take 100+ ticks
any > 10 Structurally fragile Do NOT retune scalars; re-architect

7.5 Pre-deployment gate

Before merging any harness change (prompt edit, tool addition, verification-rule change):

  1. Compute the perturbation \(\delta A\) induced by the change (re-run the trace on the modified harness, re-estimate \(A'\), set \(\delta A = A' - A\)).
  2. Check \(\|\delta A\|_W \leq \eta^*\) from §7.3.
  3. If it exceeds the budget, the change is blocked pending a full re-diagnosis.

That’s a 30-second check. And it catches the exact class of regressions that currently require a full sprint to debug. You’ll feel like a wizard.

8. Discussion, Limitations, and Open Problems

8.1 Limitations

# Limitation Scope Mitigation
1 Linearization validity. The Jacobian \(A\) is only accurate within a neighborhood of \(b^*\). For large initial transients (cold-start, new task), the eigenmode decomposition is qualitative, not quantitative. Ticks 1–5 of a new task Use the bound as a lower bound on convergence time; expect actual convergence to be slower.
2 Stochasticity. The framework applies to the mean-field operator. Per-tick sampling noise (temperature > 0, tool-call retries) adds an effective diffusion term that can mask a small Δ. All ticks with \(T_{\text{emp}} > 0\) Average over \(\geq 10\) stochastic runs to estimate \(\bar{A}\); report the variance of Δ across runs.
3 Non-normality. If \(A\) is far from normal (\(\kappa(A) \gg 1\)), the eigenvector-based convergence bound is loose and transient “pseudospectral” growth can dominate for the first few ticks. Harnesses with strongly coupled tool chains Use the pseudospectral abscissa \(\alpha_\epsilon(A)\) instead of \(\rho(A)\); see Trefethen (2005).

8.2 Interaction with the categorical framework

The functorial autonomy framework treats safe refactors as natural transformations. In the language of this post, a natural transformation \(\eta: F \Rightarrow G\) between two harness functors induces a block-matrix perturbation

\[\delta A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \eta \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\]

on the combined state space. The spectral-conditioning algorithm (§6) can be constrained to respect this block structure, ensuring that the “conditioning” is itself a safe refactor. This closes the loop between the categorical safety criterion and the spectral performance criterion. Neat, right?

8.3 Open problems

  • Time-varying harnesses. If the harness configuration changes mid-run (adaptive prompts, dynamic tool sets), \(A\) becomes a product \(A_N A_{N-1}\cdots A_1\). The relevant object is the Lyapunov exponent, not the eigenvalue. Extending the gap to the joint spectral radius is an active area (Blanchard & Cohen, 2011). Nobody’s fully solved this yet, to my knowledge.
  • Multi-agent coupling. When two harnesses interact (e.g., a planner harness and an executor harness), the combined operator is a Kronecker sum, and the gap is the minimum of the individual gaps. This suggests a “weakest link” principle for multi-agent autonomy. Makes sense intuitively, at least.
  • Learned conditioning. Can the SDP in §6 be replaced by a learned policy that maps \((\Delta, \kappa_{\max}) \mapsto \delta A^*\)? If so, the harness could self-condition at runtime, closing the loop from the gauge-symmetry classification to real-time adaptation. This is where it gets really exciting.

9. Conclusion and Next Steps

The spectral gap Δ isn’t a new invariant of autonomy. It’s a diagnostic—one that translates the vague, qualitative question “is my harness converging?” into a quantitative, pre-deployment check with a hard numerical threshold. Combined with the eigenmode condition numbers, it separates the two fundamentally different failure modes—parametric drift (fixable by retuning) and structural fragility (requiring re-architecture)—and gives you a certificate you can literally attach to a pull request.

This framework sits inside the broader architecture we’ve been building across this series. The autonomy spectrum defines what autonomy is. The rate-distortion bound defines how much information it costs. The percolation model defines when it emerges. The free-energy bound defines how much you may spend per tick. The geodesic formulation defines the path between autonomy states. And the spectral gap defines the speed along that path—and how fragile that speed is to the changes you’re about to make.

If you have a harness in production, start with Step 1 of Section 7 today. You need a state logger, a 50-tick trace, and a call to numpy.linalg.eig. Everything else—the SDP, the certificates, the conditioning algorithm—is optional until you’ve seen your own \(\lambda_2\) and your own Δ. Once you have those two numbers staring back at you in a terminal window, the rest of the machinery in this post tells you exactly what to do with them. And it’ll click.
