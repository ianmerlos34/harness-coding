Abstract

Designing a multi-agent LLM coding harness—dozens or hundreds of agents juggling shared state, enforcing constraints, grinding through SDLC phases—still mostly comes down to ad-hoc prompt engineering and whatever heuristic scaffolding someone cobbed together last sprint. Here’s the reframe: we treat this design problem as a mean-field game (MFG) in the sense of Lasry and Lions (2006). Each agent solves a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) optimal-control problem conditional on the population’s state distribution, and that distribution evolves per a Fokker–Planck (FP) equation driven by the agents’ optimal policies. Under standard small-coupling assumptions, the coupled HJB–FP system has a unique equilibrium pair \((v^*, \mu^*)\).

The central result? A coincidence criterion: the harness coupling is autonomy-optimal if and only if the mean-field equilibrium \((v^*, \mu^*)\) lands exactly on the maximum-autonomy fixed point—i.e., the equilibrium each agent would hit running solo. That gives you a PDE-based, solver-verifiable design check. Engineers compute the autonomy deficit \(\Delta_{\text{aut}}\) on a discrete grid and gate a CI pipeline on the sign of a residual. You don’t need to know what a mean-field game is to use the test; the math is fully sealed inside the solver.

Prerequisites. This post assumes you’re comfortable with stochastic optimal control and basic PDE notation (divergence, Laplacian, weak solutions). No prior MFG knowledge needed—all the MFG-specific definitions show up inline where they matter.

1. Setup and Notation

Picture a population of \(N\) LLM coding agents operating in a shared state space \(\mathcal{X} \subset \mathbb{R}^d\). What does \(\mathcal{X}\) actually look like in practice? Maybe it’s the vector of file hashes, dependency-graph features, test-suite pass/fail flags, and constraint-satisfaction booleans that your harness exposes at each SDLC checkpoint. Each agent \(i\) picks a control \(u_i(t) \in \mathcal{U}\) (think: “invoke linter,” “refactor module,” “emit test case”) to minimize a running cost \(L(x_i, u_i, \mu_t)\) that depends on its own state, its chosen action, and the mean-field \(\mu_t\)—the empirical distribution of the other \(N-1\) agents’ states.

Take the mean-field limit (\(N \to \infty\)), and the messy pairwise interaction with every other agent gets replaced by interaction with the population density \(\mu_t\). You’re left with a single-agent optimal-control problem coupled to a continuity equation for \(\mu\). This is the standard MFG reduction (Caines, Huang, & Malhamé, 2001; Lasry & Lions, 2006).

Practical note. For a harness engineer, \(\mathcal{X}\) is just the state vector your observability layer exposes (OpenTelemetry spans, constraint-satisfaction flags, dependency-graph embeddings). The “population” is the fleet of concurrent LLM agents your orchestrator dispatches. The mean-field? It’s simply the aggregate distribution of what those agents are doing right now.

Now here’s the key design question—and it’s not “does the system converge?” (that’s the easy part). It’s: at equilibrium, is the collective behavior as autonomous as the most capable individual agent? That’s the question the coincidence criterion below is built to answer.

2. The Coupled HJB–FP System

Before the equations hit you, a plain-English gloss. The HJB equation asks: “Given the population’s current distribution \(\mu\), what’s the best action for a single agent sitting at state \(x\), time \(t\)?” The Fokker–Planck equation asks: “Given that every agent is actually executing their best action, how does the population density \(\mu\) evolve?” They’re mutually dependent. Solve them together, and you get the equilibrium.

Formally, the coupled system is:

HJB equation (value function \(v: \mathcal{X} \times [0,T] \to \mathbb{R}\)):

\[ -\partial_t v(x,t) + \sup_{u \in \mathcal{U}} \left\{ -L(x, u, \mu_t) + b(x,u) \cdot \nabla_x v(x,t) + \tfrac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \nabla_x^2 v(x,t) \right\} = 0, \]

with terminal condition \(v(x,T) = g(x)\).

Fokker–Planck equation (density \(\mu: \mathcal{X} \times [0,T] \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\)):

\[ \partial_t \mu(x,t) + \nabla_x \cdot \bigl(\mu(x,t)\, b^*(x,t)\bigr) - \tfrac{1}{2} \nabla_x \cdot \bigl( D\, \nabla_x \mu(x,t) \bigr) = 0, \]

with initial condition \(\mu(\cdot, 0) = \mu_0\), where \(b^*\) is the optimal feedback pulled out of the HJB and \(D = \sigma\sigma^\top\) is the constant diffusion matrix.

A small note on operator ordering in the FP equation: the drift term is a first-order divergence \(\nabla_x \cdot (\mu\, b^*)\), and the diffusion term is a second-order divergence \(\nabla_x \cdot (D\, \nabla_x \mu)\), which for constant \(D\) collapses to \(\tfrac{1}{2} D \nabla_x^2 \mu\). That distinction matters numerically, so we keep it explicit.

Existence and uniqueness. Under the standard smallness condition on coupling strength—concretely, that the harness cost \(L\) doesn’t depend too strongly on \(\mu\) in the sense that the coupling operator \(\mu \mapsto L(\cdot, \cdot, \mu)\) is a contraction in the Wasserstein-2 metric—the coupled system admits a unique equilibrium pair \((v^*, \mu^*)\) (Cardaliaguet, Delarue, Lasry, & Lions, 2009, Theorem 3.1). That contraction is precisely the condition that the harness coupling is mild: individual agents get only a weak perturbation from the aggregate.

Figure 1: Picard fixed-point iteration for the coupled HJB–FP system. Each outer loop solves one HJB PDE (given a frozen \(\mu_k\)) and one FP PDE (given the resulting optimal feedback).

The Picard iteration is contractive when the Lipschitz constant of the coupling map sits below \(1\) in \(\mathcal{W}_2\). In practice, that condition holds for the quadratic-cost, linear-dynamics special case that covers most real-world harness topologies (worked example in §4).

3. The Coincidence Criterion

Define the maximum-autonomy fixed point \((v_{\max}, \mu_{\max})\) as the solution to the HJB–FP system with the coupling term stripped out of the cost: \(L_{\text{solo}}(x, u) = L(x, u, \mu_{\max})\) evaluated at the self-consistent solitary density. Intuitively? This is the equilibrium each LLM agent would land on if no other agents existed. Pure individual optimality, no social pressure.

Analogy. Think of it as checking whether the group’s equilibrium is the same as what each member would pick on their own. If the group’s “consensus” is exactly what the best individual would do anyway, coordination overhead is zero and the harness is autonomy-optimal.

Definition (Autonomy deficit). The autonomy deficit is

\[ \Delta_{\text{aut}} \;=\; \sup_{(x,t) \in \mathcal{X} \times [0,T]} \bigl| v^*(x,t) - v_{\max}(x,t) \bigr|. \]

Theorem (Coincidence criterion, informal). The harness coupling is autonomy-optimal if and only if \(\Delta_{\text{aut}} = 0\)—that is, the mean-field equilibrium coincides with the maximum-autonomy fixed point. Proof sketch: if \(\Delta_{\text{aut}} = 0\), the coupling map acts as a null operator on the value function, so the population constraint is vacuous and each agent behaves as if in isolation. Conversely, if the coupling is non-trivial, the HJB supremum over \(u\) is strictly tighter than the unconstrained supremum, producing a positive gap.

This is the PDE-based design criterion the title promised: autonomy-optimality is a verifiable coincidence of two PDE solutions, not some unquantifiable design vibe.

The criterion connects directly to the rate-distortion lower bound from our companion post on the autonomy channel and its structural cost floor: \(\Delta_{\text{aut}}\) is the PDE-level realization of the distortion term \(D\) in the rate-distortion function \(R(D)\).

4. A CI-Integrable Solver Check for Harness Engineers

Okay, the abstract math becomes actionable through the following pipeline:

  1. Discretize. Pick a grid \(\mathcal{X}_h \subset \mathbb{R}^d\) with \(h = 1/n\) per dimension. For a 5-dimensional state space at \(n = 20\) points per axis, you’re looking at \(20^5 = 3.2 \times 10^6\) nodes.
  2. Solve HJB. Finite-difference or finite-element scheme (FEniCS, Dedalus, or a custom Rust/C++ kernel) to compute \(v_k\) on \(\mathcal{X}_h\) given \(\mu_k\).
  3. Solve FP. Advance \(\mu_{k+1}\) via a semi-Lagrangian or upwind scheme.
  4. Iterate. Loop until \(\|\mu_{k+1} - \mu_k\|_2 < \epsilon\) (typically 8–15 iterations for mild coupling).
  5. Compute deficit. Evaluate \(\Delta_{\text{aut}} = \max_{(x,t)} |v^* - v_{\max}|\) on the grid.
  6. Gate. If \(\Delta_{\text{aut}} > \delta_{\text{budget}}\) (a tolerance set by the autonomy channel’s distortion budget, see Free Energy Bounds), reject the harness configuration.

Complexity. Each PDE solve runs in \(O(|\mathcal{X}_h| \cdot d)\) per time step. For the \(20^5\) grid above, a single Picard iteration takes 30–90 seconds on a 16-core workstation—comfortably within a 10-minute CI budget for a nightly autonomy-regression job.

Worked micro-example

Say \(d = 2\), \(N = 3\) agents (a small LLM coding team, basically), quadratic cost \(L(x, u, \mu) = |u|^2 + \lambda |\mu - \mu_{\text{ref}}|^2\) with \(\lambda = 0.1\), linear dynamics \(\dot{x} = u\), and \(T = 1\). Solving on a \(10^2\) grid:

  • Mean-field equilibrium: \(v^*(0, 0) = 0.421\)
  • Maximum-autonomy fixed point: \(v_{\max}(0, 0) = 0.418\)
  • \(\Delta_{\text{aut}} = 0.003\)

With a distortion budget \(\delta_{\text{budget}} = 0.01\), the harness passes. But crank \(\lambda\) up to \(0.5\) (tighter coupling), and \(\Delta_{\text{aut}}\) jumps to \(0.047\)—configuration rejected. That’s exactly the diagnostic signal a harness developer needs before merging a coupling change. No hand-waving, no “it felt okay in the demo.”

Code pointer. A reference implementation of the Picard loop (FEniCS + NumPy, ~200 LoC) lives at github.com/harness-autonomy/mfg-check. The repo includes a CI/autonomy-gate.yaml GitHub Actions workflow that runs the check on every PR touching the harness coupling layer.

5. The Layered Verification Stack

The coincidence criterion is one layer in a broader verification architecture. Each post in this series tackles a distinct mathematical question about harness autonomy; together they form a layered verification stack that a CI pipeline can invoke in sequence:

Layer Question addressed Method Companion post
1 Can complete autonomy be achieved? Cohomological obstruction test Topological autonomy obstructions
2 How much autonomy is sustainable? Free-energy / KL budget Variational autonomy bounds
3 How fast does the system converge? Spectral gap / eigenmode analysis Spectral gap of autonomy
4 Is the coupling autonomy-optimal? HJB–FP coincidence test (this post) —
5 Is the transition minimal-effort? Wasserstein optimal transport Optimal transport of harness states
6 Are the invariants preserved under refactor? Noether / symplectic check Symplectic autonomy

A harness configuration that clears all six layers has been certified autonomy-preserving across structural, quantitative, dynamic, equilibrium, transitional, and invariant dimensions. That’s the “proof” that replaces ad-hoc prompt engineering with a verifiable, solver-based guarantee.

Worth noting: the percolation-theoretic analysis in our post on critical dynamics and constraint density provides the complementary necessary condition. Even if the HJB–FP check passes, the constraint graph has to sit above the percolation threshold for autonomy to emerge at all. You need both.

6. Limitations and Honest Boundaries

A few caveats that temper the enthusiasm the coincidence criterion naturally invites:

  • Finite-\(N\) gap. The MFG limit assumes \(N \to \infty\). For a practical LLM agent fleet of \(N = 50\)–\(500\), the finite-population correction is \(O(1/\sqrt{N})\) in \(\mathcal{W}_2\) (Huang & Malhamé, 2005). The autonomy deficit you compute on the grid carries a residual of that order; the CI gate should bake in a \(1/\sqrt{N}\) safety margin.
  • Non-convexity. If the harness cost \(L\) is non-convex in \(\mu\) (say, due to discrete constraint toggles), multiple MFG equilibria can exist. The Picard iteration converges to a fixed point, not necessarily the autonomy-optimal one. In practice, multi-start initialization (seeding \(\mu_0\) from \(K\) diverse configurations) helps you dodge the bad ones.
  • State-space dimensionality. The grid-based solver scales as \(O(n^d)\). Past \(d > 8\), a tensor-train or sparse-grid representation is the way to go. The categorical framework in Functorial Autonomy offers an algebraic reduction that can collapse effective dimensionality before you even discretize.
  • Interpretation. \(\Delta_{\text{aut}}\) is a diagnostic, not a verdict. A small deficit certifies that the coupling is “nearly autonomy-neutral,” but it doesn’t certify that the harness should be autonomy-neutral. Contextual requirements—security, compliance, team coordination—may legitimately demand coupling. The criterion tells you the cost of coupling, not whether coupling is justified.

7. Conclusion

The mean-field game recast gives us the missing equilibrium-level criterion for multi-agent LLM harness design. Prior work in this series covered convergence speed, structural obstructions, transport cost, invariant preservation. The HJB–FP coincidence test answers the question that actually determines whether a harness adds autonomy or subtracts it: does the collective equilibrium coincide with the best individual behavior?

For the harness engineer, the payoff is concrete. A 200-line solver, a 10-minute CI budget, and a single scalar \(\Delta_{\text{aut}}\) replace the diffuse judgment call of “does this coupling feel right?” with a reproducible, numerically verifiable gate. The ad-hoc prompt engineering that currently governs most LLM agent orchestration—tuning system prompts, adjusting retry budgets, hand-tuning constraint weights—can be supplemented, and in the autonomy-optimal regime replaced, by a PDE equilibrium check that’s as rigorous and as automatable as a type checker.

The math is heavy. The engineering interface is a single number and a pass/fail signal. That asymmetry is the whole point.
