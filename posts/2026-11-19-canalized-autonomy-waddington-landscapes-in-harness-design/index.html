Abstract

So here’s the question that keeps coming up in harness design: how many constraints do I actually need to keep the agent productive without over-determining it? We recast this as a geometry problem on a Waddington epigenetic landscape, derive a computable canalization depth metric δ, and present a neutral-network traversal algorithm that safely removes constraints while preserving basin integrity. The framework applies directly to any LLM agent scaffold under active development.

Key Takeaways

  • Canalization depth δ is a single scalar summarising how robustly a harness channels agent behaviour toward productive outputs, independent of the agent’s specific trajectory.
  • Complete autonomy (no hard constraints) corresponds to a landscape with a single global attractor basin; its geometry is computable from the harness specification alone.
  • Neutral-network traversal gives a step-by-step algorithm for removing one constraint at a time, with a verifiable invariant that the agent’s effective autonomy does not decrease.
  • The metric and algorithm are implementable today via constraint-satisfaction solvers (Z3, OR-Tools) and fit naturally into CI pipelines.
  • The framework unifies and contextualises the rate-distortion, spectral, percolation, and information-geometric results developed earlier in this series.

Where This Fits in the Series

This post is the geometric backbone of the harness-design series. Earlier entries establish what autonomy is (the five-level spectrum), how much information must flow through the harness (rate-distortion), and how fast it converges (spectral gap). Here we ask what shape the constraint space must have so that autonomy is robust rather than brittle. 1. The Intuition: A Valley, Not a Fence

Waddington’s 1942 epigenetic landscape (Waddington, 1942) depicts cellular differentiation as a ball rolling down a crinkled surface: the folds are developmental constraints, the valleys are attractor states, and the ball’s final position is determined jointly by the terrain and the initial push. The crucial observation is that the ball does not need to be told where to go; the shape of the landscape does the steering.

A coding-agent harness is, in structural analogy, the terrain. The agent is the ball. The constraints—tool-whitelists, output-schema validators, retry budgets, permission gates—are the folds in the surface. A well-designed harness is not a cage; it’s a valley that guides the ball toward productive basins while leaving lateral freedom within the valley floor.

In plain terms: You don’t need to micromanage every function call. You need to shape the “ground” so that the agent’s natural momentum carries it toward correct outputs, and the sides of the valley prevent catastrophic deviations.

Now, this intuition isn’t exactly new. The dialectical framing establishes that scaffolding is the generative precondition of autonomy, not its tax. The percolation analysis shows that autonomy emerges in discrete jumps as constraint density crosses a critical threshold. What’s missing—and what this post is actually about—is a geometric account of how deep the valleys are and how to safely smooth them.

2. Formal Setup: The Harness as a Landscape

Okay, so we’ve got the picture. Now let’s fix notation so the geometric objects are actually precise.

Let \(\Sigma\) be the finite set of hard constraints in a harness specification (tool-whitelists, schema validators, permission gates, retry caps, etc.). For a constraint \(c \in \Sigma\), let \(\Phi_c : \mathcal{A} \to [0,1]\) be a restriction functional measuring the fraction of the agent’s action space \(\mathcal{A}\) that \(c\) eliminates. The harness potential is

\[\Phi_\sigma(\mathbf{a}) \;=\; \sum_{c \in \sigma} \Phi_c(\mathbf{a}), \qquad \sigma \subseteq \Sigma.\]

The Waddington landscape associated with \(\sigma\) is the pair \((\mathcal{A},\, \Phi_\sigma)\), where \(\mathcal{A}\) is (for analytical convenience) a compact Riemannian manifold with boundary, and \(\Phi_\sigma\) is a smooth, non-negative function whose zero set \(\mathcal{Z}_\sigma = \{\mathbf{a} : \Phi_\sigma(\mathbf{a})=0\}\) is the set of permitted agent states.

Remark (Why a manifold?). In practice \(\mathcal{A}\) is discrete (a finite set of tool calls, API endpoints, file paths). We embed it in a smooth manifold to access differential-geometric machinery—Hessian eigenvalues, basin volumes, curvature—without loss of fidelity. The embedding is via the information-geometric lift described in a companion post; the resulting metric tensor \(G\) is computable from the harness specification.

In plain terms: \(\Phi_\sigma\) is a “penalty score” for every possible agent action. Zero means “allowed”; large means “blocked.” The shape of this penalty surface is the harness.

Agent dynamics under the landscape are modelled by the overdamped Langevin SDE

\[d\mathbf{a}_t = -\nabla\Phi_\sigma(\mathbf{a}_t)\,dt + \sqrt{2\kappa}\,d\mathbf{W}_t,\]

where \(\kappa > 0\) is an effective “temperature” capturing the agent’s stochasticity (sampling randomness, context-window noise) and \(\mathbf{W}_t\) is standard Brownian motion on \(\mathcal{A}\).

In plain terms: The agent drifts downhill (toward low-penalty actions) while being kicked around by noise. The depth of the valley determines how far the noise can push it before it escapes.

3. The Complete-Autonomy Limit

What about the extreme case \(\sigma = \varnothing\) (no hard constraints)? You get \(\Phi_\varnothing \equiv 0\): a flat landscape. And that’s not the autonomy we want—unconstrained drift is undirected. The meaningful complete-autonomy regime is \(\sigma = \{c_{\min}\}\), a single soft constraint (e.g., “produce valid JSON”) that defines a unique global minimum \(\mathbf{a}^*\).

Proposition 1 (Single-basin geometry). If \(\Phi_{\{c_{\min}\}}\) is strictly convex on \(\mathcal{A}\), the landscape possesses exactly one attractor basin \(\mathcal{B}^*\), and the basin volume \(\text{Vol}(\mathcal{B}^*)\) is computable from the Hessian \(\nabla^2\Phi_{c_{\min}}(\mathbf{a}^*)\) via the Gaussian approximation

\[\text{Vol}(\mathcal{B}^*) \;\approx\; (2\pi)^{d/2}\,|\nabla^2\Phi(\mathbf{a}^*)|^{-1/2}.\]

In plain terms: If the “valley” has a single, well-defined bottom and the sides are monotonically rising, there’s exactly one place the ball ends up, and we can calculate how wide the valley is from the curvature at the bottom.

This is the geometric content of the free-energy bound: the basin volume is the agent’s effective autonomy, and the Hessian determinant encodes the structural cost floor identified in the rate-distortion analysis.

4. Canalization Depth: A Robustness Metric

Here’s the central contribution: the canalization depth \(\delta(\sigma)\), defined as the minimum “energy barrier” an agent must surmount to escape the productive basin \(\mathcal{B}^*\):

\[\delta(\sigma) \;=\; \min_{\mathbf{a} \in \partial\mathcal{B}^*} \;\Phi_\sigma(\mathbf{a}) \;-\; \min_{\mathbf{b} \in \mathcal{B}^*}\;\Phi_\sigma(\mathbf{b}).\]

Equivalently, \(\delta\) is the depth of the valley measured from rim to floor.

In plain terms: \(\delta\) answers the question “how hard is it for the agent to accidentally do something outside the productive range?” A large \(\delta\) means the valley is deep—robust. A small \(\delta\) means the agent is one noisy step from escaping into unproductive territory.

Properties.

  1. Monotonicity: If \(\sigma' \supseteq \sigma\), then \(\delta(\sigma') \geq \delta(\sigma)\). Adding constraints can only deepen the valley.
  2. Computability: For piecewise-quadratic \(\Phi_\sigma\) (the common case when constraints are linear-inequality or box constraints), \(\delta\) reduces to a linear programme over the constraint normals. For general \(\Phi_\sigma\), it’s a convex optimisation problem solvable to any \(\epsilon\) in polynomial time (assuming the landscape is a convex function on \(\mathcal{A}\), which holds for the standard constraint families).
  3. Invariance: \(\delta\) is invariant under the gauge transformations that reparameterise the harness without changing the induced agent dynamics. Thus \(\delta\) is a genuine invariant of the autonomy structure, not an artefact of a particular encoding.

In plain terms: You can rephrase your constraints, rename your tools, reformat your prompts—as long as the effective restriction on the agent’s action space is the same, \(\delta\) does not change. It measures the geometry, not the syntax.

5. Neutral-Network Traversal: Safely Simplifying the Harness

So the practical question every harness designer eventually hits is: which constraint can I actually remove without letting the agent escape the basin? This is exactly the neutral-network problem from evolutionary biology (Kauffman & Levin, 1987; Kauffman, 1993): a neutral step is a mutation that changes the genotype (here, the constraint set) without changing the phenotype (here, the basin \(\mathcal{B}^*\) and the canalization depth \(\delta\)).

Definition. A constraint \(c \in \sigma\) is neutral with respect to \(\sigma\) if

\[\mathcal{B}^*(\sigma \setminus \{c\}) = \mathcal{B}^*(\sigma) \quad\text{and}\quad \delta(\sigma \setminus \{c\}) \geq \delta(\sigma) - \varepsilon,\]

for a designer-specified tolerance \(\varepsilon > 0\).

5.1 Traversal Algorithm

Each “Reject” branch preserves the current harness; the algorithm only commits when both invariants (basin identity and depth tolerance) hold. The procedure terminates because \(\Sigma\) is finite.

Correctness sketch. By induction on \(|\sigma|\): the base case \(|\sigma|=1\) is the single-basin proposition. The inductive step relies on the fact that removing a neutral constraint is a natural transformation in the category of harnesses—i.e., it preserves all autonomy invariants simultaneously.

5.2 Worked Example (Three Constraints)

Suppose the harness \(\sigma = \{c_1, c_2, c_3\}\) contains:

Constraint Type Effect
\(c_1\): tool-whitelist Hard Agent may only call read_file, write_file, run_test
\(c_2\): output-schema Hard Final response must be valid JSON matching schema \(S\)
\(c_3\): retry-budget Soft At most 3 tool-call retries per task

Measured values: \(\delta(\sigma) = 0.72\) (arbitrary normalisation).

Step 1. Test \(c_3\). Remove it: \(\delta(\sigma\setminus\{c_3\}) = 0.71\). Basin unchanged. \(0.71 \geq 0.72 - 0.05\). Neutral → commit.

Step 2. Test \(c_2\). Remove it: \(\delta(\sigma\setminus\{c_2\}) = 0.34\). Basin fragments (agent may now emit free text). Reject → keep \(c_2\).

Step 3. Test \(c_1\). Remove it: \(\delta\) drops to 0.11; basin vanishes. Reject → keep \(c_1\).

Result: \(\sigma^* = \{c_1, c_2\}\). The retry budget was redundant; the other two constraints are load-bearing. The agent retains full lateral freedom within the JSON-producing, whitelisted-tool basin.

In plain terms: You tried removing each constraint one at a time. The retry limit turned out to be decorative—the agent was already well-behaved without it. The tool whitelist and the output schema, however, are structural: remove either, and the agent’s behaviour scatters.

5.3 Implementation Note

In practice, you encode \(\Phi_\sigma\) as a constraint-satisfaction problem in Z3 or OR-Tools and compute \(\delta\) via a sequence of linear programmes (one per constraint boundary facet). The neutral-test loop is a simple for-over-constraints with a solver call per iteration. A reference implementation for the three-constraint example above is ~40 lines of Python and fits in a CI job that runs on every harness-spec change. This connects directly to the CI-auditable refactoring plan derived in the Wasserstein companion.

6. Practical Considerations and Limitations

Click to expand caveats and edge cases

The ball is not point-like. The Langevin model treats the agent as a single state vector. A real LLM agent has a context window, a tool-call history, and a multi-step plan. These are higher-order state variables that the one-dimensional landscape does not capture. The RG-flow analysis provides the coarse-graining prescription: integrate out fast variables (individual tokens) to obtain an effective landscape on the slow manifold (task-level decisions). The \(\delta\) computed on the coarse-grained landscape is the one that matters for design.

Strict convexity is a modelling assumption. Real harnesses often produce non-convex \(\Phi_\sigma\) (e.g., “either use the REST API or the gRPC API, but not both” creates a disconnected feasible set). In that case \(\delta\) becomes a minimum over multiple basins, and the neutral-network algorithm must be run per-basin. The cohomological obstruction theorem characterises when such disconnectedness is topologically unavoidable versus a parametric artefact.

Temperature \(\kappa\) is agent-specific. The same harness yields different \(\delta\)-effective values for different LLMs (different sampling temperatures, different context-lengths, different instruction-following fidelity). The \(\delta\) metric is harness-relative; cross-model comparison requires calibrating \(\kappa\) empirically, e.g., via the spectral-gap measurement protocol.

The landscape is not static. As the agent accumulates context, the effective \(\Phi_\sigma\) shifts. This is the percolation regime: the landscape dynamically crinkles as information enters the context window. The traversal algorithm should therefore be re-run at each major SDLC phase boundary (spec → implement → test → deploy), not just once.

The \(\delta\) metric subsumes the rate-distortion floor (a small \(\delta\) implies a small channel capacity), is compatible with the spectral-gap convergence bound (deep valleys converge faster), and is invariant under the gauge and functorial equivalences. The cohomological result shows that \(\delta = 0\) is not merely a design failure but can be a topological necessity—the landscape has a hole that no amount of parameter tuning can fill.

8. Conclusion: The Geometry of the Valley

Waddington sketched his landscape on a napkin in 1942, arguing that development is not programmed step-by-step but channeled by the shape of the available state space. Eighty years later, the same geometry governs the harnesses we build around large language models. The constraint is not a command; it’s a fold in the terrain. The agent’s autonomy is not the absence of topography but the geometry of the valley: its depth (\(\delta\)), its width (basin volume), its convergence rate (spectral gap), and its topological type (cohomology).

The canalization-depth metric and the neutral-network traversal algorithm together give the harness designer a computable, CI-auditable, geometry-based procedure for the central practical question: what can I safely take away? And the answer is not “as much as possible” but “as much as the valley depth tolerates,” and the tolerance is a number, not a judgement call.

If you’re building a harness this quarter: start by enumerating your constraint set \(\sigma\), sketch the basin topology (convex or not?), compute \(\delta\) with a 40-line Z3 script, and then run the traversal loop. You’ll likely find that one or two constraints you added for peace of mind are neutral—removing them simplifies the harness and increases the agent’s effective autonomy, with zero loss in robustness.
