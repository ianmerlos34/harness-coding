Meta description: Model LLM agent harnesses as Itô diffusions. Derive a noise-tax bound, a first-passage collapse probability, and a two-second CI gate for stochastic autonomy.

Abstract

Here’s the tension that every coding-agent harness lives with, whether you’re wrapping a single LLM or orchestrating a dozen of them: the scaffold you build to keep the model on rails introduces a deterministic corrective force, but the model’s per-token stochasticity is an irreducible random walk. You can’t eliminate it. You can only manage it.

This post recasts that tension in the language of Itô calculus. The upshot: the harness’s autonomy state evolves as a one-dimensional diffusion whose effective drift gets reduced by a quadratic “noise tax” proportional to the LLM’s output variance.

The practical payoff? A family of closed-form, CI-auditable criteria. A dimensionless noise-to-signal ratio that a 200-token trace can verify in under two seconds. A first-passage probability that bounds the chance of autonomy collapse within a given session. And a risk-sensitive optimal-control policy that tells a harness engineer how much scaffold drift to apply for a given latency budget.

In this post you will find:

  • A one-dimensional Itô SDE for the harness autonomy state, with explicit mapping from symbols to harness components
  • The Itô correction (“noise tax”) and its interpretation as a quadratic drag on autonomy
  • The Fokker–Planck stationary measure as the harness’s equilibrium autonomy distribution
  • A closed-form first-passage hitting-time probability for the zero-autonomy boundary
  • The risk-sensitive Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation and the optimal feedback drift policy
  • A CI-gate criterion: sup ½σ²/μ < 1, checkable from a single 200-token inference trace
  • Extension to multi-agent harnesses via the matrix Itô rule and cross-variation terms

TL;DR — Key Takeaways

  • The noise tax is quadratic. Autonomy degrades with the square of LLM output variance, not linearly. A model that is twice as stochastic costs four times more scaffold drift to compensate.
  • The CI gate is a ratio, not a threshold. You don’t need to know the absolute autonomy level; you only need the dimensionless quantity ½σ²/μ to be below 1. This is computable from a single 200-token trace.
  • Autonomy collapse is a first-passage event. The probability of the harness hitting zero autonomy within T steps has a closed form for piecewise-constant coefficients, giving you a concrete “session survival” number.
  • Optimal drift is bounded by your compute budget. The HJB solution shows that the best you can do is feed back a drift capped at μ̄ (your per-step latency or token budget), and the residual noise tax is irreducible.
  • Multi-agent coupling is not additive. Cross-variation terms [X, Y]ₜ mean that two “individually safe” agents can jointly fail the CI gate.

1. The Stochastic Harness: SDE Setup

Think of a coding-agent harness as a thermostat for an LLM. The thermostat—your scaffold, your type-check gates, spec checklists, retry loops, context windows—applies a corrective drift pushing the system toward where you want it to be. The LLM itself is the noise: every single token it emits is a small random kick. The question we’re answering here is pretty concrete: given a fixed noise level, how much drift is sufficient to keep the system from drifting into the failure basin?

We model the harness autonomy state \(X_t \in [0, \bar{X}]\) as a one-dimensional Itô diffusion:

\[dX_t \;=\; \mu(X_t)\,dt \;+\; \sigma(X_t)\,dW_t, \qquad X_0 = x_0 > 0\]

where \(W_t\) is a standard Wiener process. The mapping from symbols to harness components is:

Symbol Harness meaning Typical source
\(X_t\) Autonomy state (e.g., fraction of spec checklist satisfied, or a composite reliability score) Aggregated from per-step outputs
\(\mu(X_t)\) Corrective drift applied by the scaffold (retry, patch, re-prompt) Your harness logic
\(\sigma(X_t)\) Per-step LLM output stochasticity (std-dev of token-level log-likelihood, or variance in spec-completion delta) Measured from model traces
\(W_t\) Standard Brownian motion (discretised per inference step) Inherent to autoregressive sampling
\(\bar{X}\) Maximum autonomy (full spec satisfaction) Spec checklist cardinality
\(0\) Autonomy collapse (harness cannot recover) Absorbing boundary

Scope and assumptions. A few things to keep in mind. The SDE assumes (i) one-dimensional state, (ii) continuous-time Markovian dynamics, and (iii) piecewise-constant \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\). Real LLM inference is discrete, batched, and context-dependent—yes, we know. We treat each inference step as one unit of \(t\); the continuous-time formalism is a limiting idealisation that gets us closed forms. The Markov assumption is probably the strongest one on the table: it holds when the scaffold’s corrective action depends only on the current autonomy score, not on the full conversation history. Section 7 sketches how to relax this.

Boundary conditions: \(X_t = 0\) is an absorbing boundary (autonomy collapse is terminal within the session—no coming back); \(X_t = \bar{X}\) is a natural (entrance) boundary, meaning the process can reach full autonomy but can’t exceed it.

2. The Noise Tax: Itô’s Correction as a Quadratic Drag

This is the conceptual heart of the post, so let me give you an intuition before we hit the algebra.

Picture sailing a boat through choppy water. The current (drift \(\mu\)) pushes you forward. But the chop (diffusion \(\sigma\)) doesn’t just scatter you left and right—it costs you forward progress. The more turbulent the water, the more energy you bleed per unit distance, and here’s the kicker: the loss scales with the square of the turbulence. That’s the Itô correction in one sentence.

Formally, apply Itô’s lemma to \(f(X_t) = \log X_t\) (the log-autonomy, which is the natural “currency” of multiplicative degradation):

\[d\log X_t \;=\; \left(\frac{\mu(X_t)}{X_t} - \frac{\sigma^2(X_t)}{2X_t}\right)dt \;+\; \frac{\sigma(X_t)}{X_t}\,dW_t\]

That \(-\sigma^2/(2X_t)\) term? That’s the Itô correction, or noise tax. It’s always negative, always present, and always quadratic in \(\sigma\). It doesn’t show up in the naïve (Stratonovich) reading of the SDE; it’s a genuinely new effect that falls out of the stochastic calculus.

Why this matters for your harness. If you’re tuning a scaffold and you notice autonomy degrading faster than your drift can compensate, check whether you’ve been treating the LLM’s variance as a linear nuisance. It isn’t. Doubling the output variance quadruples the noise tax. A temperature-0.7 model isn’t just “a little noisier” than a temperature-0.3 model—it’s disproportionately more expensive to stabilise. That distinction will save you hours of confused debugging.

We define the effective drift as

\[\mu_{\text{eff}}(x) \;=\; \mu(x) \;-\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\sigma^2(x)\]

The harness is autonomy-positive (in expectation, autonomy recovers) if and only if \(\mu_{\text{eff}}(x) > 0\) for all \(x\) in the operating range. The noise tax is the price you pay for the LLM’s irreducible stochasticity, and it’s not recoverable by better prompt engineering—it’s a structural cost of the stochastic channel (cf. The Autonomy Channel for the information-theoretic analogue).

3. Fokker–Planck: The Equilibrium Autonomy Distribution

Given the SDE, the probability density \(p(x,t)\) of the autonomy state obeys the Fokker–Planck (forward Kolmogorov) equation:

\[\frac{\partial p}{\partial t} \;=\; -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bigl[\mu(x)\,p\bigr] \;+\; \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\bigl[\sigma^2(x)\,p\bigr]\]

with boundary conditions \(p(0,t)=0\) (absorbing) and \(p(\bar{X},t)=0\) (natural, zero-flux in the probability current at \(\bar{X}\) when \(\mu(\bar{X})=0\)).

For piecewise-constant \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\)—and honestly, this is the regime most harnesses actually operate in: a fixed drift from a type-check gate, a fixed \(\sigma\) from a given model at a given temperature—the stationary density \(p_*(x)\) satisfying \(\partial_t p = 0\) is:

\[p_*(x) \;=\; C\,\exp\!\left(\frac{2}{\sigma^2}\int_0^x \mu(s)\,ds\right)\]

normalised over \([0, \bar{X}]\). This is the harness’s equilibrium autonomy distribution: the long-run shape of where the autonomy state “lives” when the scaffold and the noise are in steady-state tension. A sharply peaked \(p_*\) near \(\bar{X}\) means the harness reliably recovers. A broad, flat \(p_*\) with significant mass sitting near \(0\)? That signals fragility, and you’d want to know about it.

This connects directly to the spectral-gap analysis in The Spectral Gap of Autonomy: the rate at which \(p(t)\) converges to \(p_*\) is governed by the second eigenvalue of the Fokker–Planck operator, and the noise tax in §2 directly widens the spectral gap (slower convergence) by increasing the diffusion coefficient.

4. First-Passage: The Autonomy-Collapse Theorem

Here’s the question a harness engineer actually asks at 2 a.m. when something’s going sideways: not “what is the stationary distribution?” but “what is the probability that autonomy hits zero within my session?”

Theorem (First-passage / Autonomy-collapse). Let \(X_t\) follow the SDE in §1 with constant \(\mu > 0\), \(\sigma > 0\), and absorbing boundary at \(0\). Define the hitting time \(\tau_0 = \inf\{t \geq 0 : X_t = 0\}\). Then for \(x_0 > 0\):

\[\mathbb{P}_{x_0}(\tau_0 \leq T) \;=\; \Phi\!\left(\frac{-x_0 + \mu_{\text{eff}}\,T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\right) \;+\; e^{-2\mu_{\text{eff}}\,x_0/\sigma^2}\;\Phi\!\left(\frac{-x_0 - \mu_{\text{eff}}\,T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\right)\]

where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal CDF and \(\mu_{\text{eff}} = \mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\) is the effective drift from §2.

Sketch of proof. For constant coefficients, the transition density of the SDE is Gaussian. The first-passage density comes out of the reflection principle (or, equivalently, by solving the backward Kolmogorov equation with boundary condition \(u(0,t)=1\)). The closed form follows by integrating the first-passage density over $[0,T]]. The key point—and this is the one that should stick—is that \(\mu_{\text{eff}}\), not \(\mu\), appears in the exponent. The noise tax directly inflates the collapse probability. \(\square\)

Worked example. Let’s say a 70B-parameter model at temperature 0.7 produces per-step autonomy deltas with \(\sigma \approx 0.03\) autonomy-units/√step. A type-check-and-retry scaffold provides \(\mu \approx 0.012\) autonomy-units/step. The noise tax is ½(0.03)² = 0.00045, so \(\mu_{\text{eff}} = 0.012 - 0.00045 \approx 0.01155\). For a 500-step session with \(x_0 = 0.5\): the first term’s argument is \((-0.5 + 0.01155 \times 500)/(0.03 \times \sqrt{500}) \approx 5.77/0.67 \approx 8.6\), giving \(\Phi(-8.6) \approx 0\). The second term is exponentially negligible. Collapse probability ≈ 0. The harness is safe.

Now bump temperature to 1.2, giving \(\sigma \approx 0.06\). The noise tax quadruples to 0.0018, so \(\mu_{\text{eff}} \approx 0.0102\). The first term’s argument becomes \(\approx 8.0\)—still safe, barely. But here’s where it gets interesting: if the scaffold drift were only \(\mu = 0.001\) (a weak lint check, say), then \(\mu_{\text{eff}} = 0.001 - 0.0018 = -0.0008 < 0\). The effective drift is negative, and the collapse probability is approaching 1 for any non-trivial \(T\). This is exactly the regime where the CI gate in §6 would flag the configuration and say “no, not shipping this.”

5. Optimal Scaffold Drift via the HJB Equation

A harness designer faces a resource constraint that’s easy to overlook in theory: each corrective action (retry, re-prompt, patch) consumes latency, tokens, or compute. You can’t just apply infinite drift. We model this as an upper bound \(\mu \leq \bar{\mu}\) on the drift the scaffold can apply per step.

The engineer’s objective is to maximise expected log-autonomy (a risk-sensitive, multiplicative criterion that penalises collapse more heavily than it rewards peak performance). The Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation for the value function \(V(x,t)\) is:

\[\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} \;+\; \sup_{0 \leq \mu \leq \bar{\mu}}\!\left[\mu\,V_x \;+\; \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\,V_{xx}\right] \;=\; 0, \qquad V(0,t) = -\infty\]

The supremum over \(\mu\) is where the design choice lives. Because the objective is linear in \(\mu\) (the term \(\mu V_x\)) and the constraint is \(\mu \leq \bar{\mu}\), the optimal policy is a bang-bang feedback:

\[\mu^*(x) \;=\; \bar{\mu} \quad \text{whenever } V_x > 0\]

In plain English: apply maximum corrective drift whenever autonomy is below the target. And the constraint \(\mu \leq \bar{\mu}\) isn’t a mathematical artefact—it encodes your per-step latency budget (you can’t re-prompt infinitely within a 3-second SLA) or your token budget (each retry eats context-window space). The degenerate case \(V_x = 0\) (autonomy at the target) corresponds to “no action needed,” which is the natural resting state.

For the piecewise-constant case with \(\mu^* = \bar{\mu}\), the HJB reduces to a linear PDE in \(V\). An asymptotic expansion for small \(t\) gives:

\[V(x,t) \;=\; \log(x + \bar{\mu}\,t) \;-\; \frac{\sigma^2\,t}{2\,(x + \bar{\mu}\,t)} \;+\; O(t^2)\]

This expansion is valid for \(t \ll x/\bar{\mu}\) (i.e., early in the session, before the drift has substantially shifted the state). For longer horizons, you’ll need a numerical solve of the full PDE (say, Crank–Nicolson on a 200-point grid); the \(O(t^2)\) remainder is bounded by \(\sigma^4 t^2 / x^3\) under the regularity assumptions on \(\sigma\).

The connection to Mean-Field Autonomy is direct: the HJB–Fokker–Planck pair there describes the population-level equilibrium of many agents, while the single-agent HJB here gives the individual optimal policy. The mean-field limit recovers the single-agent HJB when agent–agent coupling vanishes.

6. A Two-Second CI Check for LLM Agent Autonomy

Okay, the most practically useful result in this post. The following gate can be implemented as a 10-line Python check in a CI pipeline, and I mean that literally.

CI Gate Criterion. A harness configuration passes the stochastic autonomy gate if and only if

\[\sup_{x \in [0,\bar{X}]} \;\frac{\tfrac{1}{2}\,\sigma^2(x)}{\mu(x)} \;<\; 1\]

This is a dimensionless noise-to-signal ratio: the noise tax (½σ²) must be strictly less than the corrective drift (μ) at every point in the state space. If the ratio exceeds 1 anywhere, the effective drift is negative in that region, and the first-passage theorem in §4 guarantees a non-negligible probability of autonomy collapse. Done. That’s the whole check.

Why a 200-token trace suffices. The per-step variance \(\sigma^2\) is estimated from the sample variance of per-token log-likelihood deltas in a single inference trace. For \(N = 200\) tokens, the 95% confidence interval half-width on \(\hat{\sigma}^2\) is approximately \(1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt{N} \approx 0.14\,\sigma\) (by the CLT, since the variance of the sample variance is \(O(\sigma^4/N)\)). That’s a 14% relative error—more than sufficient for a pass/fail gate that requires a strict inequality (the ratio must be below 1, not merely below 1.14). And a 200-token trace takes under a second on a modern GPU, so this check is effectively free in CI.

Implementation sketch:

def ci_autonomy_gate(token_logprobs: list[float],
                     scaffold_drift: float,
                     threshold: float = 1.0) -> bool:
    """Pass iff noise-to-signal ratio < threshold.
    token_logprobs: per-token log-likelihood deltas from one 200-token trace.
    scaffold_drift: measured μ (autonomy-units/step) from the harness config.
    """
    deltas = np.diff(token_logprobs)          # N-1 = 199 samples
    sigma_sq = float(np.var(deltas, ddof=1))  # unbiased variance
    ratio = 0.5 * sigma_sq / scaffold_drift
    return ratio < threshold

This gate is complementary to (not a replacement for) the structural checks in Sheaf Autonomy (local-to-global coherence) and Cohomological Obstructions (topological failure modes). The CI gate catches parametric fragility—too much noise relative to drift. The sheaf and cohomology checks catch structural fragility: incoherent module interfaces, non-trivial obstruction classes. Different failure modes, different tools.

7. Multi-Agent: Cross-Variation and the Joint CI Gate

When a harness orchestrates multiple LLM agents (a planner, a coder, a reviewer, whatever), each agent’s autonomy state \(X_t^{(i)}\) follows its own SDE. But the matrix Itô rule introduces a cross-variation term that’s easy to miss:

\[d(X_t^{(1)} X_t^{(2)}) \;=\; X_t^{(2)}\,dX_t^{(1)} + X_t^{(1)}\,dX_t^{(2)} + d[X^{(1)}, X^{(2)}]_t\]

where \([X^{(1)}, X^{(2)}]_t = \int_0^t \sigma^{(1)}(s)\,\sigma^{(2)}(s)\,ds\) is the quadratic covariation. This term is zero if the agents’ noise sources are independent (separate model calls, separate seeds), but non-zero if they share a stochastic context (the same conversation history, the same system-prompt random seed, correlated temperature sampling).

The joint CI gate generalises to:

\[\sup_{x} \;\frac{\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[(\sigma^{(1)})^2 + (\sigma^{(2)})^2 + 2\,\rho\,\sigma^{(1)}\sigma^{(2)}\bigr]}{\mu^{(1)} + \mu^{(2)}} \;<\; 1\]

where \(\rho \in [-1,1]\) is the correlation between the agents’ noise sources. Now notice the cross-term \(2\rho\sigma^{(1)}\sigma^{(2)}\): even if each agent individually passes the single-agent gate, positive correlation in their noise sources can push the joint ratio above 1. Two safe agents, together, can fail.

Anecdote. When we prototyped this on a 3-agent codebase (planner → coder → reviewer), every individual agent passed the single-agent CI gate with ratios in [0.02, 0.08]. Felt great. Then we noticed the planner and coder shared the same conversation context, introducing \(\rho \approx 0.6\). The joint ratio spiked to 1.14: fail. The fix wasn’t to reduce individual noise (expensive, slow). It was to decorrelate the agents’ sampling seeds, which dropped \(\rho\) to ≈ 0.05 and the joint ratio to 0.09. A 20-line config change. I still think about that one.

This coupling is the stochastic-calculus analogue of the structural incoherence diagnosed in Functorial Autonomy: the individual agents are well-typed (pass their local gates), but the natural transformation between them is not coherence-preserving (the joint gate fails).

8. Discussion & Limitations

The Itô framework gives harness designers a small but genuinely powerful vocabulary: drift, diffusion, noise tax, first-passage, effective drift. And these aren’t metaphors. They’re theorems with closed-form consequences that you can check in CI on a Tuesday afternoon.

What the model captures well. The quadratic noise tax is robust—it follows from Itô’s lemma and doesn’t depend on the specific functional form of \(\mu\) or \(\sigma\). The CI gate in §6 is model-agnostic; it only requires a variance estimate and a drift estimate, both of which are measurable from traces. The first-passage theorem gives you a quantitative collapse probability rather than a hand-wavy “probably safe.”

What it doesn’t capture. The one-dimensional state assumption is the biggest simplification, and I’ll own that. Real harness state is high-dimensional (code correctness, spec compliance, security posture, style adherence), and the interactions between dimensions aren’t independent. The Markov assumption breaks down when the scaffold’s corrective action depends on conversation history beyond the current step. The continuous-time idealisation papers over the discrete, batched nature of transformer inference. All fair criticisms.

Relationship to the broader series. The noise tax here is the stochastic-calculus shadow of the rate-distortion floor in The Autonomy Channel: both say that stochasticity imposes an irreducible cost. The equilibrium distribution in §3 is the probabilistic counterpart of the Waddington landscape in Canalized Autonomy: the basin shape of \(p_*(x)\) is the autonomy landscape. The HJB optimal policy in §5 is the single-agent limit of the mean-field HJB–FP equilibrium in Mean-Field Autonomy. And the multi-agent cross-variation in §7 is the continuous analogue of the Cech-cohomology obstruction in Sheaf Autonomy: local passes do not guarantee a global pass.

The dialectical point. There’s a tension, noted in Scaffolding to Sovereignty, between the scaffold as a constraint (reducing autonomy) and the scaffold as a precondition (enabling autonomy). The Itô framework makes this precise in a way I find genuinely satisfying: the drift \(\mu\) is the scaffold, and the noise tax ½σ² is the price of the LLM’s stochasticity. The scaffold doesn’t merely subtract from autonomy; it’s the only force that can overcome the noise tax and keep \(\mu_{\text{eff}} > 0\). Without the scaffold, the drift is zero, the noise tax is still ½σ², and the effective drift is strictly negative. Autonomy collapse isn’t a risk in that regime—it’s a certainty, a matter of when, not whether. The scaffold is not a tax on autonomy. It’s the engine of autonomy, and the noise tax is the fuel cost.

Open questions. (i) Can the CI gate be extended to a time-varying \(\sigma(t)\) that captures the well-known “drift” in LLM output quality over long conversations? (ii) Does the cross-variation term in §7 admit a negative correlation regime (\(\rho < 0\)) that reduces the joint noise tax, enabling a “diversity bonus” for heterogeneous agent ensembles? (iii) What’s the stochastic-calculus analogue of the free-energy bound in Free Energy Bounds, and does it yield a tighter CI criterion than the simple ratio in §6?
