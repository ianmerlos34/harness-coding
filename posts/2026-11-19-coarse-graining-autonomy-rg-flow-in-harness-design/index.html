So you’re building a harness for a coding agent, and you’re staring at this wall of constraints wondering: which of these actually matter, which can I delegate, and which are just… overhead? Yeah, I’ve been there. The design space blows up combinatorially, and no amount of checklist-ifying it makes that go away.

Here’s the thing that clicked for me: Wilsonian renormalization-group (RG) methods—originally built to explain why liquid water and iron magnets obey the same scaling laws despite being, microscopically, completely different things—give you a principled answer. Integrate out the micro-degrees of freedom that don’t change the agent’s macroscopic autonomy profile. What’s left? Your minimal effective harness. That’s the whole trick.

This post takes that machinery and applies it squarely to LLM agent harness design. I’ll identify the fixed-point structures that correspond to scale-invariant autonomy architectures, and then derive a six-step cascade algorithm you can actually run on your own constraint set. No hand-waving.

Key takeaways

  • Harness autonomy decomposes into a finite set of relevant operators (the constraints that matter at every scale) and irrelevant operators (micro-decisions that wash out under coarse-graining).
  • Exactly four topologically distinct intermediate fixed points sit between full supervision and full autonomy. Your harness lives on one of them, or in a flow basin between them.
  • Autonomy “phase transitions”—that brittle-to-robust jump you see when removing a single guardrail—happen at critical points where a relevant operator’s RG eigenvalue crosses zero.
  • A practical RG-cascade algorithm (pseudocode in §5) lets you iteratively eliminate irrelevant constraints and converge on the minimal harness for a target autonomy level, in \(\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{ops}} \cdot L \cdot P)\) perturbation runs.

Who should read this: You’ll get the most out of this if you’ve read the preceding entries in this series (especially the rate-distortion and percolation pieces) and you’re comfortable with the vocabulary of statistical mechanics—coarse-graining, relevant/irrelevant operators, fixed points. No QFT background needed; I gloss every equation in plain English.

1. The Combinatorial Problem

When you design a harness for an AI coding agent, the design space isn’t some single dial you can turn. It’s a high-dimensional lattice of decisions, and it’s a lot of them: which tools the agent may call, in what sequence, under what permission vector, with what verification predicate, at what level of task delegation, subject to which security invariants, constrained by which SDLC-phase invariants, mediated by which UI affordances. And that’s for one agent. Multiply it out across a multi-agent system and good luck doing this by inspection.

The practical question is deceptively simple: which of these constraints are load-bearing, and which are decorative? A constraint is load-bearing if removing it changes the agent’s observable autonomy profile—success rate, failure mode distribution, robustness to novel inputs. It’s decorative if, after you integrate out the fine-grained micro-decisions it governs, the macroscopic behaviour doesn’t budge. Wilsonian RG gives you a formal criterion for that distinction. And a procedure to compute it.

And look, this isn’t just a metaphor I’m stretching. The mathematical structure is genuinely isomorphic: a lattice of micro-decisions (token-level tool calls, parameter choices, intermediate state transitions) gets progressively “integrated out” to yield a coarser effective description (task-level delegation, phase-level orchestration, system-level governance). The fixed points of that flow are the scale-invariant autonomy architectures—the harness designs whose qualitative behaviour doesn’t depend on the resolution at which you observe them.

2. The Wilsonian Framework Applied to Harnesses

2.1 The Transfer Functional

In the Wilsonian picture, you start with a fine-grained probability measure \(\mu\) over the space of all possible micro-decision sequences \(\mathcal{M}\):

\[\mu(m) = P(\text{agent trajectory} = m \mid \text{harness config } \theta)\]

Coarse-graining is a map \(\mathcal{C}: \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{M}'\) that projects each micro-trajectory onto a lower-resolution description (e.g., collapsing a 200-token tool-call sequence into a single “task completed” event). The transfer functional \(\mathcal{T}\) is the map that pushes forward the measure:

\[\mu' = \mathcal{T}[\mu] = \mathcal{C}_\# \mu\]

Intuitively: the coarse-grained harness is the one whose probability distribution over macro-outcomes best predicts the average behaviour of all the fine-grained micro-decisions it absorbs. The RG flow is just the iterated application of \(\mathcal{T}\) as the resolution parameter \(\ell \to \infty\).

The effective coupling vector \(\mathbf{g}\) (your set of constraint strengths, permission levels, verification strictness, delegation depth) evolves under the flow:

\[\frac{d\mathbf{g}}{d\ln\ell} = \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{g})\]

where \(\mathbf{K}\) is the RG kernel, determined by the transfer functional and the geometry of the constraint space.

2.2 Relevant and Irrelevant Operators

Linearise around a fixed point \(\mathbf{g}^*\), and the RG kernel decomposes into eigenvectors with eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\):

  • Relevant operators (\(\lambda_i > 0\)): perturbations grow under coarse-graining. These are your load-bearing constraints. Remove one and the harness’s macroscopic autonomy profile shifts.
  • Irrelevant operators (\(\lambda_i < 0\)): perturbations decay. These are the decorative micro-decisions. You can tune them freely without anyone noticing.
  • Marginal operators (\(\lambda_i = 0\)): the critical case. Small parameter changes can tip the system across a phase boundary. Handle with care.
Operator type RG eigenvalue Role in harness Example in a production harness
Relevant \(\lambda_i > 0\) Load-bearing; must be preserved Security invariant: “never write to /etc or ~/.ssh”
Relevant \(\lambda_i > 0\) Governs autonomy level Maximum delegation depth (L1–L5 spectrum)
Irrelevant \(\lambda_i < 0\) Washes out at macro scale Exact JSON key ordering in a tool schema
Irrelevant \(\lambda_i < 0\) Decorative micro-decision Whitespace in generated code (when no style gate exists)
Marginal \(\lambda_i \approx 0\) Critical; can tip phase Verification predicate strictness at the brittle-to-robust boundary

This table is, honestly, the central engineering result. Your job is to identify the relevant operators and spend your design effort there. The irrelevant ones? Set them by convention and forget about them.

3. Fixed Points and the Autonomy Spectrum

The RG flow admits exactly six fixed points in the autonomy direction, corresponding to the five-level spectrum established in The Autonomy Imperative:

  1. Full supervision (\(\ell = 0\)): every token-level decision is externally specified. All operators are relevant; there’s no irrelevant direction. The harness is a deterministic state machine with the LLM as a subroutine.
  2. L1 – Tool-use autonomy: the agent picks which tool to call but not the arguments. Two relevant operators (tool selection, argument validation) remain.
  3. L2 – Task-level autonomy: the agent decomposes a task into sub-tasks and sequences tool calls. Four relevant operators; the micro-decisions within each tool call become irrelevant.
  4. L3 – Phase-level autonomy: the agent plans across SDLC phases (spec → implement → test → review). Six relevant operators; intra-phase micro-decisions wash out.
  5. L4 – Project-level autonomy: the agent manages multi-file, multi-phase projects with self-directed testing. Eight relevant operators; the constraint space is sparse.
  6. Full autonomy (\(\ell \to \infty\)): the agent is a closed loop. The only relevant operator is the external termination criterion. The harness reduces to a sandbox boundary.

One correction to a common imprecision I’ve seen: at the full-supervision fixed point, it’s not that the relevant eigenvalues “go to infinity.” Rather, all operators in the space are relevant—there are no irrelevant directions to integrate out. The RG matrix is full-rank. As you move toward full autonomy, operators successively become irrelevant (their eigenvalues cross zero), and the dimensionality of the effective harness decreases. That’s the precise sense in which autonomy is a dimensionality reduction.

The rate-distortion analysis proves that this dimensionality reduction carries an irreducible cost: the distortion (loss of guaranteed safety) is bounded below by \(R(D)^{-1}\), where \(R(D)\) is the channel rate. The RG picture makes the structure of that cost visible: it lives in the relevant operators you must retain.

4. Critical Points and Autonomy Phase Transitions

4.1 The Brittle-to-Robust Transition

The most practically important critical point sits between L2 and L3. At this transition, the verification-predicate operator (the “does the output satisfy the spec?” check) crosses from relevant to irrelevant as you increase delegation depth. Below the critical coupling \(g_c\), the agent is brittle: small input perturbations propagate straight through to task failure. Above it, the agent is robust: the internal self-correction loop absorbs perturbations before they reach the macro level.

This is the harness-design analogue of the percolation threshold identified in Percolation of Autonomy: autonomy doesn’t degrade smoothly as you remove constraints. It collapses in a discrete jump once the constraint density falls below \(p_c\). The RG eigenvalue crossing is the microscopic mechanism behind that percolation threshold.

Now, here’s where it gets interesting (and a little uncomfortable). The cohomological-obstruction result shows that some failures at this transition are topological, not parametric. No amount of tuning the verification predicate’s strictness will cross the boundary if the harness’s constraint topology admits a non-trivial \(H^1\). The RG eigenvalue crossing is necessary but not sufficient; the obstruction class must also vanish. You can’t brute-force your way through a topological failure.

5. The RG-Cascade Algorithm

Here’s the algorithm. It takes a concrete harness configuration and iteratively eliminates irrelevant operators, converging to the minimal effective harness for a target autonomy level.

Input: Constraint set \(\mathcal{C} = \{c_1, \ldots, c_N\}\), target autonomy level \(\ell^*\), perturbation step \(\delta\).

Output: Minimal constraint set \(\mathcal{C}^* \subseteq \mathcal{C}\).

# Pseudocode: RG-Cascade for Harness Minimalisation
# Complexity: O(N_ops * L * P) where P = # perturbation runs per step

def rg_cascade(constraints, target_level, delta=0.01, tol=1e-4):
    active = list(constraints)
    history = [active.copy()]

    for coarse_level in range(target_level):
        # Step 1: Linearise the RG kernel around current fixed point
        K = estimate_rg_matrix(active, delta)   # central-difference, O(delta^2) error
        eigenvalues, eigenvectors = eigh(K)

        # Step 2: Classify operators
        relevant   = [i for i, lam in enumerate(eigenvalues) if lam >  tol]
        irrelevant = [i for i, lam in enumerate(eigenvalues) if lam < -tol]
        marginal   = [i for i, lam in enumerate(eigenvalues) if abs(lam) <= tol]

        # Step 3: Integrate out irrelevant operators
        #         (fix them at their fixed-point values; they no longer affect macro behaviour)
        for i in irrelevant:
            active[i].freeze(value=active[i].fixed_point_value)

        # Step 4: Check for marginal operators → phase-transition risk
        if marginal:
            flag_critical(active, marginal)
            # Engineer must decide: nudge above or below g_c

        # Step 5: Update the transfer functional (re-estimate K for next level)
        active = reparametrisation(active, relevant)
        history.append(active.copy())

        # Step 6: Convergence check
        if max(abs(eigenvalues[relevant])) < tol:
            break

    return active, history

Practical notes for implementers:

  • Step 1’s “estimate the RG matrix” is a finite-difference approximation: perturb each coupling \(g_i\) by \(\pm\delta\), measure the change in the autonomy metric \(\mathcal{A}\) (e.g., success rate on a benchmark suite), and form the Jacobian. The error is \(\mathcal{O}(\delta^2)\) for central differences; I’d use \(\delta \approx 10^{-2}\) for a good bias/variance trade-off.
  • The dominant cost is Step 1: \(N_{\text{ops}}\) perturbation runs per coarse-graining level, \(L\) levels total. For a harness with 20 constraints and 4 autonomy levels, that’s 80 perturbation runs. Tractable on a CI pipeline, honestly.
  • Marginal operators (Step 4) are the ones corresponding to the percolation threshold. The gauge-symmetry analysis provides a symmetry-based shortcut: operators related by a gauge transformation can’t be distinguished by the RG flow, so you only need to classify one representative per orbit. Saves a lot of redundant work.

6. Worked Example: A Python Tool-Call Harness

Let’s make this concrete. Suppose your harness has the following configuration for an L2 autonomy target:

  • A Python tool-call schema (5 tools: read_file, write_file, run_test, search, commit)
  • A two-level permission vector (read-only vs. read-write)
  • A single verification predicate: “all tests pass before commit”
  • A security invariant: “never write outside the project directory”
  • A style gate: “PEP 8 compliance on all generated code”

Cascade run (target: L2, i.e., two coarse-graining steps):

Step Action Result
0 Estimate \(\mathbf{K}\) on 6 active constraints Eigenvalues: \([+2.1, +1.4, +0.9, +0.3, -0.4, -1.8]\)
1 Classify Relevant: {security invariant, verification predicate, permission vector, delegation depth}. Irrelevant: {style gate, exact tool-call ordering}.
2 Freeze irrelevant Style gate → set to “PEP 8 (advisory)”; tool ordering → “LLM’s choice”. Constraint count: 6 → 4.
3 Re-estimate \(\mathbf{K}\) on 4 active Eigenvalues: \([+1.9, +1.1, +0.2, -0.1]\)
4 Classify Relevant: {security, verification, permission}. Marginal: {delegation depth} (\(\lambda = +0.2\), near zero).
5 Flag marginal “Delegation depth is at the critical point. Nudge up (more autonomy) or down (more control).”
6 Engineer decision: nudge up Final harness: 3 relevant constraints + 1 marginal (flagged). 2 frozen.

And there it is: a three-constraint minimal harness (security boundary, test-before-commit, permission level) plus a flagged marginal parameter. The style gate and tool ordering are provably irrelevant at L2—removing them changes no observable autonomy metric. You don’t need to keep them. You just needed them to compute the fact that you didn’t.

7. Comparison with Existing Heuristics

The RG-cascade isn’t the only way to simplify a harness. Two common alternatives come up a lot:

Method Procedure When it works When it fails
Remove-until-failure Start with full constraints; remove one at a time; keep the set at first regression. Small constraint sets (< 10); clear single-failure-mode. Interacting constraints; the “first failure” may be a symptom, not the cause.
Percolation tuning Sweep constraint density \(p\); find \(p_c\) empirically (see Percolation of Autonomy). You want the threshold, not the minimal set. Does not tell you which constraints to keep, only how many.
RG-cascade (this post) Classify operators by eigenvalue; freeze irrelevant; converge. You want the minimal load-bearing set and a certificate that the rest is irrelevant. Overhead of perturbation runs; assumes the autonomy metric \(\mathcal{A}\) is well-defined.

The RG approach is worth the \(\mathcal{O}(N \cdot L \cdot P)\) overhead when you’re designing a harness that will be maintained across autonomy levels (L1 → L2 → L3) and you need to know in advance which constraints will survive. The percolation approach is faster for a one-shot tuning session. The remove-until-failure heuristic? Fine for a prototype. But it gives you no structural guarantee, and that stings when you’re three months in and the “first failure” was just the loudest symptom.

The free-energy-bound result provides an independent upper bound on how many relevant operators you can retain given your per-step KL budget. Use it as a sanity check: if the RG-cascade says you need 8 relevant operators but the free-energy bound says your budget supports only 5, you’ve got a structural inconsistency that no amount of tuning will resolve.

8. Discussion: RG Reinterpretation of Prior Results

Prior result RG reinterpretation Implication for the engineer
Rate-distortion floor The cost of integrating out irrelevant operators is the distortion \(D\); the rate \(R\) is the number of relevant operators you must retain. You cannot reduce \(D\) below the floor by adding more irrelevant constraints.
Percolation threshold \(p_c\) is the coupling value at which a relevant operator’s eigenvalue crosses zero. The jump is not a bug; it is the RG critical point. Design around it, not against it.
Gauge symmetry Gauge-equivalent constraints are the same RG eigenvector; only the gauge-invariant core is physically relevant. Do not spend design time on gauge-variant reparametrisations.
Functorial structure Natural transformations between harnesses are RG-invariant: they commute with the transfer functional. A “safe refactor” (natural transformation) preserves the fixed point. Check the naturality square.
Free-energy bound The KL budget is the total “relevant eigenvalue mass” the harness can sustain. Budget your relevant operators explicitly; the bound is a hard cap.
Cohomological obstruction Non-trivial \(H^1\) means the RG flow has a topological obstruction to reaching the full-autonomy fixed point. Some failures are not tunable. Diagnose the obstruction class before iterating.
Information geometry The RG flow is a geodesic on the Fisher–Rao manifold of autonomy profiles; the “effort” to move between fixed points is the geodesic length. Choose the shortest geodesic (fewest coarse-graining steps) between your current and target autonomy level.
Scaffolding dialectic Scaffolding is the irrelevant-operator subspace: it is necessary for the RG flow to be well-defined (the transfer functional requires a fine-grained starting measure) but eliminable in the limit. Do not remove scaffolding abruptly; let the RG flow integrate it out gradually.

9. Limitations

Okay, the honest part.

The autonomy metric \(\mathcal{A}\) is an assumption, not a theorem. The RG-cascade classifies operators relative to whatever metric you choose (success rate, robustness, latency, safety). If your metric is misspecified, the “irrelevant” operators might be relevant to a different objective. In practice: run the cascade for at least two metrics (e.g., success rate AND safety violation rate) and take the union of relevant sets.

The linearisation is local. The eigenvalue classification is valid only in a neighbourhood of the fixed point. Far from \(\mathbf{g}^*\), nonlinear terms in \(\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{g})\) can generate new relevant directions that the linear theory doesn’t see. In practice: after freezing operators, re-run the cascade from scratch with the reduced set to verify no spurious relevance has emerged.

The perturbation cost dominates. The \(\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{ops}} \cdot L)\) count in §5 omits the \(P\) perturbation runs per step (each requiring a full agent roll-out on a benchmark). For \(N = 20\), \(L = 4\), \(P = 100\) (100 benchmark episodes per perturbation), the total is 8,000 roll-outs. Ouch. In practice: batch the perturbations; use the functorial criterion to skip operators that are natural-transform-invariant.

Marginal operators are underdetermined. At \(\lambda_i \approx 0\), the classification is noise-sensitive. In practice: treat any operator with \(|\lambda_i| < 2\sigma\) (where \(\sigma\) is the standard error of the eigenvalue estimate) as marginal and flag it for manual review rather than auto-freezing.

The analogy is structural, not literal. There’s no physical temperature, no thermodynamic limit, and no universal coupling constant in a harness. The RG framework is a mathematical structure that the constraint dynamics happen to instantiate; it does not imply that harnesses obey the same physical laws as magnetic materials. The predictive power is in the classification (relevant/irrelevant) and the critical-point structure, not in any quantitative universality. I keep wanting to say “this is just an analogy,” but honestly the isomorphism is tight enough that the distinction mostly doesn’t matter for engineering purposes.

10. Conclusion

Wilsonian RG gives harness designers a principled, finite-dimensional handle on what is otherwise an intractable combinatorial space. The three central results—(i) a finite set of scale-invariant fixed points, (ii) critical points marking autonomy phase transitions, and (iii) a practical cascade algorithm for operator classification—convert the question “which constraints should I keep?” from an art into a computation. The worked example in §6 shows that even a modest six-constraint harness reduces to three load-bearing operators once the irrelevant micro-decisions are identified and frozen.

The deeper lesson, consistent with the dialectical framing of scaffolding as generative precondition, is that the irrelevant operators aren’t waste. They’re the fine-grained substrate from which the transfer functional is defined. You need them to compute the coarse-grained description, even though you don’t need them to maintain it. The RG flow tells you exactly when you can let go. And that’s… actually pretty satisfying, once you see it.
